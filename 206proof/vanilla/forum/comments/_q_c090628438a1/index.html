    SEATTLE P-I
    There will be no last-minute legal hook shot to bring back the Sonics. No courtroom dramatics between Howard Schultz and Clay Bennett.

    Schultz, the former Sonics chairman, withdrew his lawsuit against Bennett's Professional Basketball Club on Friday, thus ending the last possibility of returning the NBA franchise to Seattle.

    Schultz filed a motion for voluntary dismissal of the case with U.S. District Judge Marsha Pechman and notified members of his former ownership group, the Basketball Club of Seattle, that the legal action was being terminated.

    In a letter to his partners, Schultz said two recent rulings by Pechman made his case more difficult, and as a result, "our legal team and I no longer believe we can be successful with this litigation."

    Schultz had been seeking to unwind the 2006 sale of the team to the Oklahoma City group, hoping to get the team put into a constructive trust that would allow it to be resold to a buyer intent on returning the franchise to Seattle.

    The only remaining legal action against Bennett's group is a class-action suit filed by former Sonics season ticket holders, but that litigation doesn't seek to return the team to Seattle.

    Pechman ruled last week that the NBA would be allowed to intervene in the suit and also knocked down Schultz's efforts to split the case into two separate trials.

    Schultz was not available for comment, but a source close to the Starbucks chairman indicated that the fatal blow was the judge's ruling against "bifurcation," which would have allowed an initial trial next spring to determine whether the PBC committed fraud, followed by a second trial in the fall to come up with an appropriate remedy.

    "We had to look at this realistically," the source said. "In the absence of an interval of several months between the liability and remedy phases, which was necessary in order to craft an effective remedy, we didn't think we could accomplish the results necessary to bring the Sonics back to Seattle."

    In his letter to the BCOS partnership, Schultz said he'd also received feedback indicating that a "continuing adversarial relationship with the NBA" could be a hindrance to the city's efforts to land a future franchise.

    "Thus the prevailing wisdom of many in the Seattle community andthe advice of key members of the BCOS is that Seattle's best chance for a professional basketball franchise is to end this litigation and allow the city, state Legislature and other parties to begin the necessary fence mending with the NBA.

    "Reluctantly," Schultz wrote, "I believe we should accept this view."

    Schultz apologized for playing what he called "a part of a disappointing history that has unfolded" and said he feels accountable for accepting the assurance of Bennett's group that they would make an honest effort to find an arena solution in Seattle.

    "I regret that I accepted those assurances and am deeply sorry for the outcome," Schultz wrote. "Unfortunately, showing that the Bennett Group lied is not enough to turn back the clock and return the Sonics. As a result, I am withdrawing our lawsuit."

    Additionally, the NBA's intervention in the case meant Schultz would have been pitted not only against Bennett's group, but also the considerable wealth and strength of the league.

    In his motion against intervention, Yarmuth noted that because of a clause Schultz agreed to in the sale papers with the NBA, his client was going to be responsible for all legal fees in any lawsuit against the league or PBC, regardless of who won the suit.


    "We are pleased Mr. Schultz sees that it is neither in his nor Seattle's best interest to continue this litigation," PBC attorney Brad Keller said. "The team has left for its new home in Oklahoma City, and it's time to move on."
