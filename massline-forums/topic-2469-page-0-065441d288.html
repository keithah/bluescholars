very good questions J.  you are right to point out the diversity of hip hop listeners and the different reasons why we on this thread see the value of Blue Scholars "goin Big".  as far as people that may have counter views to alternative messages in the music, people start somewhere.  music has shown that it is a powerful thing in tapping into different feelings, emotions, and interpretations for people.  it seems to me that many that even agree with the messages have a tough time applying themselves to address the issues.  so, with the messages i am hearing in blue scholars shows the spectrum from being an "ordinary guy" to applying one's self in different ways (theory into practice). 

along the lines of listeners that listen for the beats (as many do), that is part of the blue scholars appeal.  dope music (lyrics and beats, etc.) from blue scholars makes it applicable to such a broad base.  we can't assume that people are going to take the lyrics and the message and run with it.  many may even deny it.  it seems we all have some music if not more that we like to listen to but don't agree with the message.  but, it doesn't stop us from still listening.   like chris rock said about the song "get low"...many females when asked about the lyrics and being labelled as different things, he got the response of "he ain't talking bout me".  so, with all this said and done, we see a reinforcement of many problems in our communities through these songs.  the question now becomes, will we see a positive shift in changing these problems in our community if artists like blue scholars "get Big"?

Quote from: sergio on June 04, 2007, 06:42:54 pm
if you continue to put out mixtapes and the like, listeners and fans will be more prone to looking towards their own city for talented musicians because there is more to hear. i doubt i am the only person who felt pain between the releases of the LP and the EP and bayani. part of that can be attributed to the fact that there werent very many folks putting out music from seattle and i realize that would be an incredible responsibility for yall to take the weight of constant musical supply. but the mixtape, from what i can see, incorporated so many different dudes (word to chevas) that i now can look to for more music to listen to. so i guess all im trying to say is keep the music coming.

this is an important point and i think a good way to look at how to expand a truly involved and organized fanbase: allow music like massline and the blue scholars to be an entrance to, and not a standard/aesthetic for, seattle's hip-hop community. as i see it, massline media is comprised of four hardworking musicians who have been relatively blessed with noteriety and decided to mobilize their talent into an industry that frankly doesn't exist in seattle. what DOES exist, however, is the music; there is a HUGE amount of people putting really dope music out in seattle, but due to the lack of organization and centralized community in seattle, we have satillite artists orbiting haphazardly around the city, some larger and with more exposure than others, and often fans of massline artists are simply ignorant to the other music that is thriving in this city.

as i see it, to get people more involved and better listeners of hip-hop music, it's important (and in fact, integral to any understanding of hip-hop) to foster a community, to understand that the music and the message are something to invest in (by invest i mean time/love, but you know, if you got the dollas!). this would be more BS, CM and GT-headlined shows with different seattle cats, tabling for other people to slang mixtapes at local shows, mixtapes with a really diverse sampling of seattle hip-hop (with massline representation but not necessarily production), basically massline big-upping their community and their city in order to big up themselves. not to say that they haven't already been doing this to a certain degree (like getting black anger to open at the showbox), but it's all about purposefully demonstrating to the regular blue scholars fan that there is a historical local context for the work they do, which for sure will motivate that person to think, "hey, let me take a closer listen." "and then, i shall buy hella merch that will cycle back into the community."

i got more concrete ideas but massline would have to hire me to find those out... psyche. but actually for real i'm unemployed.

people in seattle are doing it up HELLA. i'm originally from the bay and although i'm more knowledgeable about the haps up here, i can say with certainty that the quality, sincerity and innovation of seattle hip-hop is easily comparable to what's going on down south. more industrial ventures like massline are the first step.

saba, your point about seattle kids not really knowing what's up with local music is on point. i advocate for more youth programming that provide exposure to local music, community activist and general art/love work.

and in response to the "only for the beats" conversation, perhaps having a side conversation about the implicit political statements behind hip-hop music and hip-hop culture isn't appropriate here. as i see it, everyone who calls themselves a fan of rap music for the instrumentation aspects should at least be aware to how and why hip-hop culture was created and why the form, while now sometimes considered an abstract genre, is firmly rooted in society and politics. although blue scholars has-- sorry for the stranger lingo-- "crossover appeal," i don't think their music flinches from the roots of hip-hop. so, general hip-hop culture awareness is impt. for the regular kexp-in' BS fan... perhaps BS should raffle off copies of "can't stop won't stop" at shows.
