Detectives take on gangs of Seattle
Specialized police unit finds itself busier than ever

Thursday, October 5, 2006

By HECTOR CASTRO
P-I REPORTER

Saturday night and two Seattle gang detectives cruise along Rainier Avenue in an unmarked Crown Victoria they both know fools no one, with its dual spotlights and array of antennas.

A small group of teens suddenly darts across the street to a Columbia City bus stop, one shouting, "Five- O, Five-O," a general warning that police are out and a reference to old TV police show, "Hawaii Five-O."

Officers, including two with Seattle police's Gang Unit, handcuff a youth believed to be involved in a shooting. The youth was released after questioning.

By the time Detective Bobby Sevaaetasi can drive around the block to contact the kids, they have all scattered.

"You see what happens," his partner, Detective S.D. Cobane, observes. "They see the car, and they're all gone."

If it seems those unmarked cars and the black shirts emblazoned with "Gang Unit" are more visible these days, it may just be because the unit is busier than it has been in years.

"We are swamped," said Sevaaetasi, the most senior gang investigator with 14 years in the unit.

So far this year, the six detectives of the Seattle police Gang Unit have been assigned 109 cases, more than in all of last year, with more than 40 of those cases coming just during the summer.

The reasons behind the violence are no different than they have always been, detectives said. Violence erupts over drugs, money and sometimes insults and perceived insults.

And, at times, a gangbanger will end up in the wrong part of town, or "out of bounds."

Some of this year's violence has been blamed on a feud between gang members in the Central District and those in South Seattle. Investigators also speculate that a power vacuum was created late last year when authorities arrested several members of a local Crips gang, leaving younger members to jockey for power.

But gang activity isn't limited to Seattle's urban core. More and more, suburban cities from south King County and the Eastside are seeing gang-related crimes in their neighborhoods, often committed by thugs from Seattle.

Earlier this month, King County prosecutors filed murder charges against Israel Velasquez-Marquez in the shooting death of Wayne Molio'o on Sept. 9 at a Tukwila bus stop. The two were from rival gangs and simply happened to cross paths.

Also this month, Kent investigators were plagued with a series of robberies and assaults on young men and women attending impromptu street races. On Sept. 13, detectives arrested Michael Bienhoff, whose street name is Casper, in connection with one of those incidents.

During one assault, Bienhoff reportedly said: "This is how we do it in California. This is Crips."

Though Bienhoff is white, police allege he is a member of the Crips.

Sgt. John Pagel, who leads a small team of detectives in Kent, said his department hasn't even begun tracking gang-related crime. But the signs are there, he said, from increasing graffiti to more assaults and drive-by shootings.

"It probably exploded about a year ago," Pagel said. "What we have going on in terms of gang activity is probably equal to Seattle."

From the federal level to the local level, authorities are paying attention.

Last year, FBI investigators announced a major crackdown on the notorious El Salvadoran gang, MS-13. Hundreds of alleged members were arrested nationwide, and Seattle police have identified some members locally.

Also, the Seattle office of the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives announced last year the creation of a Violent Gang Task Force with agents joined by a Seattle police detective and King County sheriff's detective.

Already tackling eight investigations, their most recent headline-grabbing success came in February, when the agency announced the arrest of 16 men and women accused of affiliations with or membership in the East African Posse. Most have already been sentenced to terms ranging from five to 10 years after reaching plea deals with federal prosecutors.

The ATF also is working on a database of gang members and gangs in the Seattle region, estimated at anywhere from 30 to 60 active gangs, with gangbangers estimated in the hundreds.

    
Gang Unit Detective S.D. Cobane checks a youth's identification as he patrols the city streets Saturday looking for gang-related activity.
Last spring, the state Attorney General's Office informally queried about two dozen law enforcement agencies around the state about their gang problem and found that most felt gang activity was on the rise.

Anecdotal experience is not enough to bring action from state leaders, however, so this fall, the agency plans to bring together law enforcement officials from around the state to discuss gangs.

One goal likely will be to agree upon a definition of gang membership, with the next step being development of a statewide database of gang members.

"Then we could start measuring the problem," said Chris Johnson, policy director with the Attorney General's Office.

"We can't go to the Legislature or the governor and say, 'You need to provide additional resources for gang prevention' if we can't even tell them how to quantify the problem," he said.

In Seattle, there is likewise no definition of a gang member. But detectives know who the players are, which gangs are most active and which areas of town they claim.

The Gang Unit once boasted 28 detectives, but it was dramatically cut in 2002 and has operated with a small team of six since then.

Because Cobane spent 12 years in the East Precinct patrolling the Central District, he and Sevaaetasi tend to spend a lot of time there.

The contacts can be as simple as spotting a group of young men hanging out, as they do along South 19th Street off Madison Avenue. Though shadows shroud a porch stoop, they see three young men in the darkness. One ambles away as Sevaaetasi slows to a stop, shining a spotlight on the steps.

While Cobane chats with the two men sitting on the steps, Sevaaetasi follows the one on the phone.

But the man is behind the closed door of a nearby house before the detective can reach him. So the two spend the next several minutes simply talking with the two men on the stoop -- one they know, the other new to them.

What's valuable is the basic street intelligence the detectives gather, something that can be key in solving gang-related crimes, detectives said.

The technique seems to work for them. Out of more than 100 cases the Gang Unit has investigated this year, detectives have cleared more than 40. A few others have been cleared as "exceptional," crimes where the victims know their assailant but refuse to cooperate for fear of being labeled a snitch, losing face or because they prefer to settle the score on their own, on the street.

That's something the detectives see all too often.

"The quickest way to make a name is to shoot somebody or get shot and live," Cobane said.

Added Sevaaetasi: "Inevitably, we'll end up arresting the victim two months later for shooting the guy that shot him two months earlier."
