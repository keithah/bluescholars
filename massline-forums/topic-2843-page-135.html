Love 'em or hate 'em, the Blue Scholars got next...
by John aka "Jawn"



As far as Hip Hop goes, the Pacific Northwest doesn't receive the credit it deserves. Outside observers typically associate the rainy upper left with its inhabitants' heavy caffeine dependencies, high suicide rates, excessive heroin use and love of all things Kurt Cobain; if any rap falls into the picture, it usually took place two decades ago and involves heavy rump shakin'. Underground heavyweights like the Oldominion crew, Black Anger, Source of Labor, Silent Lambs Project, and more recent flavors like Dyme Def, Cancer Rising and Common Market have all drawn great local followings, but the national spotlight has yet to shine on our dim Emerald City.

Enter George "Geo" Quibuyen and Alexei Saba "Sabzi" Mohajerjasbi -- the duo otherwise known as the Blue Scholars, whose classic "one DJ and one emcee" formula a la Eric B. and Rakim, Pete Rock and CL Smooth, DJ Premier and Guru, Chief Xcel and Gift of Gab, and so many other legendary twosomes has propelled them to the to the top of local Hip Hop charts. Starting out as two ordinary college students at the University of Washington, they've rapidly earned their respect among thousands of Seattleites both young and old just dying to see some homegrown talent blow up. They've since shared the stage with acts like De La Soul, Immortal Technique, The Coup, Zion I, Slick Rick, and most recently Kanye West, proving that all hope is not lost in the Northwest.

June 12, not coincidentally the day when Filipinos worldwide celebrate their country's independence (read: "end-dependence") from Spanish colonial tyranny, marks the release of the Blue Scholars' third major album Bayani. Like their preceding self-titled debut and subsequent Long March EP, this full-length work intermeshes the personal and political on wax through tracks addressing issues ranging from the United States' ever unpopular misadventure in Iraq ("Back Home") to Seattle's subtle racial and class antagonisms as they occur on our friendly bus routes ("Joe Metro"). During the mean time, Geo sprinkles references to figures as diverse as the revolutionary thinker Karl Marx, Filipino American working class hero Carlos Bulosan, and even Seattle's one time favorite basketball player Xavier McDaniel (who moved on to the Phoenix Suns long before I even started watching sports), while Saba lays down his trademark "laid back" rhythms straight out the Gourmet Sabzi Kitchen.

The end result is a near-perfect mix of steady social commentary over jazzy beats (which I happen to enjoy the hell out of) worth chewing and digesting very slowly. And once again, topics range as widely as the aforementioned characters. The working immigrant's struggle to adjust to strange and hostile surroundings, coupled by his tragic disillusionment with the so-called American Dream is laid bare on "The Distance", a cut which examines the hard life as experienced by Geo's own father, but could easily represent the pain that millions of immigrants endure across the United States today. Flash forward (or back?) to the year 1999, as Geo recounts the violent clashes that took place between big business and the common folk at Seattle's Convention Center on "50K Deep", a spine tingling ode to the WTO protests that evokes images of a massive revolution sweeping the country. And of course, doesn't that encapsulate the album Bayani itself?

My number one favorite track certainly seems to suggest so. Undoubtedly the most aggressive, thumping cut on the entire album, "Fire for the People" captures the essence of Bayani (and the Blue Scholars themselves) with a constantly expanding chant -- "more fire for the people", coupled by "more jobs for the people", "more food for the people", "more life for the people," etc., each new demand addressing the basic employment, healthcare and survival needs denied to countless Americans living under King George and his Illuminazi cohorts. Much more so than that, however, the track reaches out to a broad, seemingly divided proletarian audience hungry to see change on all levels, from the purely political to the musical. As the more casual listener might stop and say, hold the jobs and the universal healthcare just a moment, Seattle hasn't heard a decent Hip Hop hit since the days of Sir Mix-a-Lot!

Northwest Hip Hop pioneers like Black Anger or the Oldominion cats might take offense upon hearing such a statement, but that unfortunately reflects the view shared by most Seattleites unfamiliar with their city's thriving underground scene. Likewise, heads across the country will readily acknowledge Hip Hop hot spots from the Bay Area to Detroit, but seldom will anyone mention forgotten, rainy ass Seatown. One really must wonder just how the Blue Scholars, a rap duo whose listening audience comprises mostly left-leaning college students and middle class white teenagers, could possibly change these hopelessly unpromising paradigms. But as a recent Seattle Times article suggests, even prominent local DJs are expecting the scene to blow up pretty soon, thanks partly to the Scholars themselves.

DJ B-Mello and DJ Hyphen of Seattle's most popular urban radio station KUBE 93.3 have both predicted releases like Bayani to generate major national noise, along with groundbreaking works by other Seattle artists like the Blue Scholars' Mass Line labelmates Common Market and Gabriel Teodros (who recently dropped his masterpiece album Lovework, given a four-star review by Okayplayer, I suggest every last one of y'all cop it), as well as Sportin' Life Records' Fatal Lucciauno (whose debut album The Only Forgotten Son will send your jaw through the floor), indie gangsta crew Dyme Def (who just dropped an equally delectable album called Space Music), and countless others I've neglected to mention. Geo and Saba couldn't have picked a better time to go national with Rawkus.

So what about Bayani? Despite the negative reviews I keep reading, I'd call it a genuine Northwest classic. It certainly possesses all the qualities of a well-rounded album, including musical and lyrical diversity as well as overall consistency. Like their previous two releases, it takes you on a sociopolitical (and personal) journey from a decidedly Marxist, proletarian perspective, while never losing sight of the fact that its listeners might not have taken the history class to know what fancy-schmancy French terms like "proletariat" mean. Its target audience consists of you and I; the ordinary schmucks barely earning enough to survive. From immigrants and wage slaves to American war veterans whose benefits the government has denied, Bayani truly seeks to inspire a broad-based coalition of folks sick and tired of the same old bullshit.

The Blue Scholars' immense local popularity has naturally lent to their growing national fame, and thanks to these efforts the whole world will soon know what truly revolutionary shit 206-Hop has to offer.
