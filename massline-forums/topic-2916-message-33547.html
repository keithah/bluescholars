Soundexchange, Pro Fusion, royalties, etc
« on: July 18, 2007, 10:22:35 am »
An article from DJ Pro Fusion about webcasting royalty rates is making it's way around the web. It contains some misinformation that leads to some bad conclusions. In the interest of people making solid business choices and coming to their own conclusions, I want to counter some of what Pro Fusion says. My comments are first below, his article is below that.

First, Soundexchange IS NOT the RIANA. Soundexchange is a non-profit Performing Rights Organization (PRO), started to collect a royalty Congress put in place called a performance royalty, which is paid for web and satellite radio play. This royalty is paid on a compulsory license (or blanket license) Internet radio stations get in order to play music without having to negotiate terms with every label for every song they play. It's very similar to the compulsory license AM/Fm radio stations must get to play songs, except that pays royalties only to the songwriter and is collected by ASCAP and BMI, not the label or the recording artist.  The royalty rate paid is set by the Copyright Royalty Board (CRB), which is part of the Library of Congress. The CRB collected testimony for years from all sorts of people to determine a royalty rate. There is debate over the new rate they have set, and whether some of the smaller web radio stations can afford it, but that's another issue, and an important one to discuss elsewhere. Anyhow, the performance royalty is collected by Soundexchange, and paid out 50% to labels and 50% to the recording artists. In this way, Soundexchange functions similarly to BMI or ASCAP. As an artist, you sign up, and when your song gets played, you are sent the royalty. Of course this royalty is tiny per song played, so you need to get significant play to make anything significant, but when you do get a decent amount of web radio play it can be another stream of income for recording artists. Now although the RIAA helped start Soundexchange, Soundexchange is not part of the RIAA. The RIAA is a member of it's board as are the 4 major labels, as are a bunch of groups representing indie labels and musicians, like The Future of Music Coalition, The American Association of Independent Music (represents indie labels), the Recording Artists Association, The Recording Academy (which has 18,000 members - mostly musicians and producers), AFTRA, American Federation of Musicians, and indie labels Matador Records and Tommy Boy. There are over 20,000 artists who are members of Soundexchange, and 2,500 indie labels. Thing is, if you are releasing albums, you should join Soundexchange if you want to get paid your royalties, as you should be a member of ASCAP or BMI. There is no fee to join Soundexchange.

So contrary to what Pro Fusion says below, the RIAA doesn't collect any royalties, and the RIAA doesn't administer the compulsory license, Soundexchange does.

I manage groups, and it's important for all groups to be on top of their business. Anytime you release an album, you should of course copyright it, as an artist you should become a member of BMI or ASCAP and as an artist or a label you should join Soundexchange.

Anyhow, I hope that clears up some of the issues. Pro Fusion is right to question anything the RIAA is involved in, as they have a spotty past. And there are lots of discussions and compromises that need to be made on the webcaster royalty rate proposed by the CRB (I think it's fair for the big guys, needs to be lowered for the small webcasters, that's me, which is what Soundexchange is offering). But for whatever discussions we have, it's important to have them starting from a basis of knowing all the relevant facts.

++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++

From DJ Pro Fusion:

There has been an understandable public outcry against the RIAA�s attempts to more than triple the sound recording copyright royalties on Internet radio. (See Save Internet Radio from Corporate Money Grab) One solution proposed by Webcasters is to just not play RIAA-member songs under the assumption that then they don�t have to pay the royalty to the RIAA�s collection body, SoundExchange; Webcasters would then just pay the independent artist the royalty.

This sounds fair and just because it is. However, the RIAA is not about being fair and just. The game is rigged and the RIAA has rigged it in their favor. The strategy of playing only non-RIAA songs won't work though because the RIAA has secured the right to collect royalties on all songs regardless of who controls the copyright. RIAA operates under the assumption that they will collect the royalties for the "sound recording copyright" and that the artists who own their own copyright will go to SoundExchange to collect at a later date.

Look at the information on SoundExchange.com (RIAA created SoundExchange) and see how it works. The RIAA has secured legal authority to administer a compulsory license that covers all recorded music.

"The recent U.S. Copyright Office ruling regarding webcasting designated SoundExchange to collect and distribute to all nonmembers as well as its members. The Librarian of Congress issued this decision with rates and terms to govern the compulsory license for webcasters (Internet-only radio) and simulcastors (retransmissions)." (http://soundexchange.com/faq.html#b4)

"SRCOs (sound recording copyright owners) are subject to a compulsory license for the use of their music...SoundExchange was established to administer the collection and distribution of royalties from such compulsory licenses taken by noninteractive streaming services that use satellite, cable or Internet methods of distribution."
(http://soundexchange.com/faq.html#a4)

SoundExchange (the RIAA) considers any digital performance of a song as falling under their compulsory license. If any artist records a song, SoundExchange has the right to collect royalties for its performance on Internet radio. Artists can offer to download their music for free, but they cannot offer their songs to Internet radio for free. (http://soundexchange.com/faq.html#a7)

So how it works is that SoundExchange collects money through compulsory royalties from Webcasters and holds onto the money. If a label or artist wants their share of the money, they must become a member of SoundExchange and pay a fee to collect their royalties (http://soundexchange.com/faq.html#b6). But, and this is a big "but," you only get royalties if you own the sound recording copyright. If you are signed to a major label, chances are you don�t. Even if you do own the copyright to your own recording of your own song, SoundExchange will collect Internet radio royalties for your song even if you don�t want them to do so.

Go to the SoundExchange site: http://plays.soundexchange.com/... and take a look at the hundreds of indie labels for whom SoundExchange claims they have collected royalties. Enter some of those label names on http://www.riaaradar.com/... and notice how few are actually members of the RIAA. Contact the label and ask if they are a member of RIAA and they almost certainly aren�t and may not even be aware that SoundExchange is collecting royalty fees on their music.

And what exactly is SoundExchange doing with the money they have collected for those hundreds of labels that must have thousands of songs???

cool, glad it helps. Read everything on this issue with a very cynical mind. Most writers are just repeating what they read somewhere else, and have little or no knowledge about this very complex issue. So there is a lot of misinformation, especially in the hip hip hop world, where some influential folks have been spreading info that isn't all correct, but sounds really bad. As with most things, take the time to educate yourself and look deeper. This is about your business if you are an artist, and so ultimately about taking care of your art, your family and yourself. Many of the people writing about this are webcasters themselves, and don't have the best interest of musicians in mind.
