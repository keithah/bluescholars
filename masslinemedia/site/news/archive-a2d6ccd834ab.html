2007-08-03 | Friday

day one in NYC was aiiiight. we took a redeye out of Seattle wednesday night and barely slept, so after waiting 3 hours in vain at JFK for the CDJ of ours that Delta Airlines lost, we took a cab to meet my cousin Omid in the Financial District in Manhattan, just 3 blocks from the Ground Zero site.

wrote a blog, watched some episodes of Flight of the Conchords on On Demand, took a nap.


(Mulch, Geologic)

woke up around 2ish, grabbed some food at the deli at the corner of Gold and Fulton and followed the music we heard coming from the pier. turned out, it was a free show by the lovely Jean Norris. anybody kno who that is?



Jean Norris was one half of the classic R&B soul duo from the early 90's, Zhane. Pronounced, Jah-nay, they supplied middle schoolers like me with some of the filthiest dance cuts including "Groove Thang" and "Hey Mr. DJ. " girls that like Common and Kangol hats loves them some Zhane.

you can see how incredibly excited we are to be there.

after that, we went to explore the Lower East Side in hopes of finding the apartment of Jermaine and Brett of the Flight Of The Conchords.  we think we found it, but we're not sure.  we're gonna go back in the daytime again and get a flick.

then, it was on the way back to Frank's Chop Shop to visit the homie Todd. Todd was the dood that laced me with the Seattle Legends New Era last time i was in NYC.
 


we had to look out and drop him off some of the new Bayani Bandanas at least... besides, Daps and Khalil wanted some Frank151 New Era collab caps that were unavailable in the SEA.


unfortunately, the caps they wanted weren't in stock at the Chop Shop either. i think Todd almost laughed at me when i asked if he still had some of the designs in stock. i guess i'm just not that hip. we copped Daps the Frank joint with the "F."
 
we closed our afternoon with another red eye.

after the Chop Shop, we met up with the homie Hollis (one half of Canary Sing; from SF by way of SEA) who's out here for 5 weeks for a program with Humanity in Action. the four of us watched The Simpsons Movie. it was funny.
 

i expect tomorrow to be much better. i'll be meeting with Hari Kondabolu in Jackson Heights to compare the dosa at a few different shops in the neighborhood.

Saba

2007-08-01 | Wednesday

It is far from my intentions to write an overly intellectualized or snooty sounding academic perspective on this interview, so I'll try to keep this short and simple.

There are a lot of folks who come up to us who say "Hey MassLiners, I really like your music because it isn't about some gangsta gangsta booty shakin bitch ho bling bling crap."  Sure, part of me appreciates this comment because, in a sense it is true, we don't like to promote destructive behavior.  I have a mom, I have a sister, I have comrades in struggle that are females; I don't want, especially as a male, to engage in or promote activity that is oppressive to my brethren/sistren.  But at the same time, when I hear these comments, another part of me feels that whoever says this is missing the point.  I certainly hope folks don't write off the valuable music and message that other artists are putting out simply because of the image they project or the language they use.  Remember, all music is political.

It's a shame that right now in hip-hop music, the promotion of one idea or image seems to necessitate an implied rejection of all other ideas and images.  Or even more so, how an artist's acceptance by a certain group of listeners seems to imply that artist's necessary rejection of other demographics.  

I believe that this is the framework folks are operating with when they pay us sideways compliments: "Their music is positive, so they'll appreciate it if I hate on some thug shit."

That is, so-called "thug shit."  I challenge anybody to listen through a Blue Scholars or Common Market record and find one line that pretentiously addresses rappers who project a different image.  I adjure them to find a single lyric that puts them down.  We've never gone out of our way to insult any artist who works hard to feed their family simply because their style is different from ours.

I personally don't think of our music as "conscious," but apparently that's the marketing angle it's picked up.  That said, so-called "conscious" music should not exclude some of the greats in the rap game now.  MCs that I personally enjoy, that most people seem to think don't count as conscious, include Lil Wayne, E-40, and TI.  All of these doods, at one point or another, have addressed very legitimate social issues in their music.  Or maybe I should say, "these mahfuccas have talked about some real shit."

Again, this isn't intended to be another pretentious academic analysis of rap music.  Pieces like that are very condescending and imply that, without an authority to translate a given rapper's lyrics into "plain english," it would have no place in civilized discussion.

I say that some of the music these rappers put out is filthy because I personally relate to the content.  Yes, some of it is party music, some of it I like because I've been conditioned to like it, some if it is plain crap, and yes, some of it is talkin about some real shit.

I'm sure I've probably said too much.  Besides that, I'm not really the best writer which is why I stick to the beats.  All this yapping is really to introduce this recorded interview of Pimp C.  Midway thru the recording he touches on some points from a perspective that is, what I feel, very similar to where Blue Scholars comes from.  Maybe just in a different style.

click here.

or go to http://www.zshare.net/audio/2833955bb6adc1/

The majority of rap/hip-hop fans like sound bites like this because they get excited and starry-eyed whenever a rapper acts hella tough; or they love to follow beef like some of these bloggers and journalists out here.  The folks that do this are only those who haven't lived real life and experienced the serious, sometimes lethal repercussions that talk like this can bring.  If I have criticism for anybody, it's these types of tourists.  

I say "thas wassup" to what Pimp C says because I am also an artist in the public eye that tries to make real music.  Furthermore, I am also an artist that has had to carefully watch the media folks that try to get us to say negative things about our friends and I'm also an artist that has had to cut fake people out of my life and business in order to protect my friends and family.

Looks like I failed at keeping it short and simple.  Oh well.  Now all the fools need to get off the booboo!  And word, I wear that same cologne too.

Saba



2007-08-01 | Wednesday


Sabzi for Dunnies:
Dawn (instrumental)





If you slept around the time Bayani dropped (ah say what, you haven't copped the album yet?) -- there was an interweb-only release/EP/radio advance/whateveryouwannacallits for the first single, Back Home. Tugging on the coattails of this first single is the 3-minute instrumental called Dawn. According to Sabzi, this didn't make it onto the album because there was no space on the album for this little diddy (no sean puffy) to play in. I'm diggin' the melodies and summer-type feel to it. And so, you long marcher you, enjoy Dawn. Maybe even put it on a burned mix CD if your partner is named Dawn. Or be really corny and play it when the sun is coming up and you're getting ready for your morning jog. Or be really productive and bump it while you're washing dishes (i'll let you figure that one out).

Sabzi - Dawn (instrumental)

Peace
