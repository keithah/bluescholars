'Idol' star boosts Blue Scholars show

By GENE STOUT, P-I POP MUSIC CRITIC

Published 10:00 pm PDT, Saturday, May 12, 2007

Blue Scholars, the idols of Seattle's hip-hop scene, got a surprise visit from "American Idol" Friday night.

Not the TV show, but Bothell-bred "American Idol" contestant Blake Lewis, who arrived on stage at The Showbox (fresh from a dizzying day of local appearances and performances) to help Blue Scholars celebrate the release of their extraordinary new album, "Bayani,"

Needless to say, the crowd went nuts. As if they weren't already in a froth over Blue Scholars' body-rocking performance.

During his unannounced appearance near the end of the show, Lewis, who showed off his remarkable beatboxing skills, revealed that he had attended junior high with Blue Scholars' DJ Alexei "Sabzi" Mohajerjasbi and that the two had performed together as teens.

RA Scion (Randy Abeo), Sabzi's partner in another hip-hop duo, Common Market, was also among the guest performers for the raucous celebration (the show repeats Saturday night at 8 at The Showbox, but whether Lewis and RA Scion will return is anybody's guess).

Backing Blue Scholars (also featuring MC George "Geologic" Quibuyen, or "Geo") were a full horn section and an electric guitarist, adding extra dimension to the duo's riveting performance, but nearly drowning out Geo's vocals at moments.

The show was well-stocked with songs from the Scholars' 2004 debut album, among them "Bruise Brothers," "Life and Debt," "No Rest for the Weary" and the pointed anti-war song, "Blink." The crowd responded with an arm-waving, body-shaking frenzy of enthusiasm (in fact, Geo asked concertgoers, who were pressing against the stage, to step back).

The spirited, insightful new songs from "Bayani," which loosely translates as "voice of the people," make this album one of the best local CDs of 2007. And it isn't hard to imagine a devoted national following for the album. The digital EP "Back Home," featuring five songs from "Bayani," was the No. 1 most-added recording on the CMJ hip-hop radio chart this week.

With its shimmering textures, poignant lyricism and "militant optimism," it's an album that Seattle can be proud of. No one has to feel guilty about grooving to this baby. It's a bling- and misogyny-free zone. "Bayani" celebrates humanity and offers hope for challenging times.

Sabzi (whose family originated in Iran) and Geo (whose parents were born in the Philippines) bring a multicultural world view to their music. The duo is like the Barack Obama of hip-hop, refreshing and inspiring rather than mean-spirited and menacing.

Friday's show offered a sampling of great songs from "Bayani," but not nearly enough. "Fire for the People" was a powerful political anthem, "North by Northwest" a tribute to hometown turf and "Morning of America" a bittersweet commentary on growing up in America. If Marvin Gaye were a young hip-hop artist living in Seattle today, he might create something like "Morning of America."

Among the more moving songs were the melancholy anti-Iraq war tune "Back Home" and the riveting WTO song, "50K Deep," in which Geo sings: "They call it a riot/ I call it an uprising."

Geo's high-impact delivery enthralled the audience. And his wide smile and joyous expression reflected the crowd's delight in sharing a special night with the stars of the Seattle's hip-hop scene. Sabzi, whose powerful beats and infectious melodies provide a backbone for the duo's music, was also clearly enjoying the good vibes.

The show wasn't just a celebration of Blue Scholars' new album. It was a tribute to hip-hop past and present, local and national, with videos from MassLine Media (Blue Scholars' Seattle label) and sets by Kidz in the Hall and Black Anger, whom Sabzi introduced as a group that had influenced Blue Scholars' own brand of hip-hop.
