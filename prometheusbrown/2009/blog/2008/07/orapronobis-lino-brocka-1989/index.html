Orapronobis/Fight For Us (Lino Brocka, 1988)

Posted on July 26, 2008
Filed Under Film Reviews, Philippine Cinema |

Orapronobis/Les Insoumis (Fight For Us) (1988) | dir. Lino Brocka | 90 min | Philippines

Imagine if Steven Spielberg, Martin Scorsese and some other Hollywood bigshots grew some balls, banded together under a political banner, and dedicated their craft, while still working within the “established” film industry, to fearlessly attack and expose government corruption and big business. As co-founder of Concerned Artists of the Philippines, Lino Brocka (1939-1991) and his colleagues did just that. At the height of his popularity, Brocka, the PI’s foremost filmmaker, took a decisive stand against the Ferdinand Marcos regime, getting a bunch of his social-realist themed films banned from theaters in the process.

The first Brocka joint I ever saw was a VHS copy of Orapronobis (Fight For Us, 1988), which the homie and kasama Daya dug up while we were both organizing with Anakbayan. At the time, I was still trying to digest the left’s criticisms of the 1986 People Power “revolution,” which I fondly remembered as a watershed moment in Philippine history and the triumphant, merciful end of a dictatorship. We didn’t know to what extent Auntie Cory continued and entrenched the same conditions that still plague the country to this day. Under the guise of “democracy,” she continued Marcos’ policy of eradicating “leftists,” “rebels” and other political opposition while keeping the neocolonial system she inherited from Marcos intact. In other words, what revolution?

Recognizing the same sobering patterns of injustice while many were still drunk with “victory,” Brocka stood up to say that even though Marcos was gone, shit was not all good. Orapronobis, perhaps Brocka’s most overtly politicized film (so I hear - I’ve only seen a few other films of his), sacrifices his usual slow-paced, multi-layered approach for something more urgent (and what some might wrongheadedly call simplistic). Filmed clandestinely and bankrolled by French producers, Orapronobis opens with a disclaimer explaining the story’s events as drawn from the real, only individual and organization names have been changed.

Similar to the true-life story of Archbishop Oscar Romero (immortalized in the film Romero), Orapronobis follows the radicalizaiton of Jimmy Cordero (Philip Salvador), a former priest turned human-rights activist, who, initially supportive of the People Power revolution under new president Cory Aquino, comes to realize that the struggle is far from over. Released from jail after Marcos’ fall, he naively defends the new government and gets long-time homie/lover/friend and fellow aktibista, Trixie (Dina Bonnevie) pregnant. Things are going great - until a right-wing relgious-fanatic vigilante group calling themselves the Ora Pro Nobis start terrorizing villagers in the coutnryside, claiming their mission to eradicate “evil” leftist rebels while the police and AFP stand by and do nothing to intervene (why not? the OPN are merely doing the job they’d be doing otherwise).

Jimmy’s new stability is short-lived. He goes on a fact-finding mission to investigate the Ora Pro Nobis’ abuses and becomes targeted by its leader, Commander Kontra (Bembol Roco). On the surface, Kontra seems an overly theatrical villain in an otherwise realistic setting. But it turns out his motivations are real and his madness is not merely for the sake of being mad. Jimmy rejects overtures from his militant comrades to join them in the countryside. That is, until tragedy strikes the family of a former sweetheart. The film’s penultimate scene (depicted on the cover), will forever remain one of the most heart-wrenching, unforgettable images embossed in my memory.

Philip Salvador, in the lead role, is a safe bet for a film whose purpose is to draw sympathy to an often misunderstood and shitted-on political position. He’s steelfaced most of the time, even in lighthearted moments, which is usually a cinematic weakness. However, I found that his reserved, everyman steez made his few flashes of emotion more impactful and relatable. He is detached and passive, just as many ordinary Filipinos, once active in the struggle, had become after People Power. Moreso, his practical approach to everything, both his strength and downfall, is what ultimately lends legitimacy and necessity to his decision to pick up the gun. He isn’t the vengeful, delusional or misguided rebel that the government would like to have us think that he, and all other militants, are like. Rather, he’s a rational man making a rational decision, perhaps not that different from you or me if either of us were in his position.

Brocka’s trademark humanism bleeds through the celluloid so much that even if you can’t understand Taglog, subtitles are almost unnecessary. You can feel your way through the film through the characters’ range of intense emotions and the power dynamics presented (between a military spokesman and a human-rights org chairperson, between the victims’ families and the police, between the Ora Pro Nobis and ordinary villagers). As straightforward as the plot is, Brocka finds room to explore deeper contradictions (between province-raised Jimmy and petty-b Trixie, between underground rebels and those in the “legal” struggle, between Commander Kontra and his own minions).

Although filmed 20 years and 3 presidents ago, human rights violations of the sort depicted in this film continue unabated under the Gloria Macapagal-Arroyo regime, made worse by US military aid and training under the guise of the “war on terror.” As more and more people join the ranks of those seeking to oust GMA, it is of primary importance that the myth of the first (and second) People Power movements are exposed, lessons drawn from them and future mistakes averted. We are fortunate to have had Lino Brocka courageously play that mythbreaker role before a tragic car accident in 1991 silenced him forever. Or so I thought.

Last year, I was fortunate to have a series of conversations with visual artist Orlando “Ka Orly” Castillo, a practitioner of social realism and a member of CAP in its early days. He reminded me that the best and most effective artists do not work in isolation, but with other artists and, most importantly, in constant contact with and drawing inspiration from the people. Brocka overstood this, and, for a director who recognizes the revolutionary potential of film, social realism is not a matter of style, but of necessity. Not on some metaphysical shit, but in true scientific and humanistic tradition, Lino Brocka’s contributions to Philippine art and culture will never be silenced.

Orapronobis/Fight For Us is one of Brocka’s better-distributed films. You might even find a copy in a chain video-rental store. Oh, and guess what? Someone made it available for download here (DivX .AVI format)!
