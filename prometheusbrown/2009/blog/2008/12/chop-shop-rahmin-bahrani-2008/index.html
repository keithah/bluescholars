Chop Shop (Ramin Bahrani, 2008)

Posted on December 23, 2008
Filed Under Film Reviews |

Chop Shop (2008) | dir. Ramin Bahrani | 84 min | USA

You don’t have to travel to a third world country (or watch a movie filmed there) to see third world conditions. Every city in this country has a slum, which we see less and less of in American films and television. Even less do we see faithful portrayals of children and workers. And less than that, children who actually work. In a year of economic downturn, everything seemed to focus on the falling standards of living for “middle class” Americans. But what about those who were already poor before the recession? Without guilt or romanticism, Chop Shop dares explore this uncelebrated and unseen side of America with a brilliance that overcomes its bare budget many times over. 

At 12-years old, Ale (Alejandro Polanco), a Nuyorican, has become a hustler by trade and necessity. We meet him, in the film’s opening shot - the youngest in a group of Latino day laborers, sitting on a busy road waiting for a chance to work. When an opportunity finally comes, he’s rejected for his age and size. Next, we see him peddling candy bars on a subway train with his friend Carlos.

Only five minutes into the film, we’ve become immersed in Ale’s post-industrial world of the Iron Triangle: Queens, New York. Working as an apprentice at an auto body shop with a hardened bunch of mechanics, he slangs bootleg porn DVDs and pulls petty licks on the side with dreams of one day starting his own business. In a small makeshift loft built from plywood, he lives with his 16-year old sister, Isamar (Isamar Gonzales), who’s later revealed to be a prostitute. For most of the film, he isn’t on a path to something greater or tragic, but going through groundhog day-like cycle of surviving and dreaming.

Unlike many established directors who have extracted the reality from verite, Iranian-American filmmaker Ramin Bahrani doesn’t rely on stylized disorientation (see Slumdog Millionaire) or gratuitous shots of urban decay to remind us how desperate the situation is (though the cinematography is stunningly vivid considering how much concrete we’re surrounded by). 

The characters, never feeling like they’re “above” their settings, carry the film, brilliantly played by a cast of nonprofessional actors most likely playing a version themselves. In fact, they all use their real names and act off a script that’s either meticulously detailed or nonexistent. What the dialogue lacks in conversational depth, it makes up for with each individual’s interaction with another. A delicate balance of tension and trust shape their every move (Ale is reprimanded for counting his pay in front of his boss). The lowest rung of the ladder in a capitalist system, they’re forced to play the game as mini-capitalists themselves, perhaps even harder than anyone else up the social ladder. They’re pitted against each other while the odds are pitted against them. 

It would be too easy to draw comparisons between Chop Shop and Italian Neo-Realist films. The influence is there, but Bahrani’s approach is more evoking of contemporary third world filmmakers such as Brillante Mendoza (Philippines) and or Abbas Kiarostami (Iran), but less contemplative and more focused on the now instead of the near future or recent past. With stunning simplicity (running only 84 minutes), Chop Shop hints at an entire unexplored realm of cinema, living in the shadows of people and places we’ve been fooled into thinking is the real America.
