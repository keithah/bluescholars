Game of Death (Robert Clouse, 1979)

Posted on February 9, 2009
Filed Under Film Reviews |

Game of Death (1979) | dir. Robert Clouse (and Sammo Hung & Bruce Lee, uncredited) | 120 min | USA

Game of Death, unworthy as it is as a complete package, carries with it a reminder that Bruce Lee, all mythologizing aside, was the real deal - a unique cinematic presence. Whatever whatever. I’m grateful that it came out, ungrateful that Quentin Tarantino had to go and make an “homage” to it with Kill Bill, and glad that I got to explain eskrima/kali to my kid when he asked about Filipino villain Danny Inosanto’s (pictured below) fighting sticks in this scene:


(that’s a classic Filipino fight face right there)


(not impressed. amused, but not impressed.)

Then Bruce proceeded to kick the shit out of him before a almost surreal fight with Kareem Abdul-Jabbar. Seriously though, a 25-minute short film featuring the five original fight scenes back-to-back would’ve been filthy enough. 

Everyone knows by now that the original Game of Death plot was something like the Kung Fu Nintendo game: Bruce Lee was supposed to fight his way up five flights of a wooden pagoda with a different “boss” on each level, each one having one weakness he must find and expose. Instead, the few filmed scenes were integrated into some cornball story about a martial-arts actor up who fakes his death to go after an organized crime team whose members happen to be the bosses from the original plot (the level-by-level fight scenes comprise the last 25 minutes of the film).

As if watching one dude kick fifty’s asses didn’t ask us to suspend disbelief enough, we’re supposed to also believe that three different stand-in actors wearing stunna shades is the same guy — Billy Lo — as the guy in the fight scenes (Bruce Lee). My four-year-old son could tell that it wasn’t Bruce less than five minutes in. Frankensteined together with Enter the Dragon director Robert Clouse’s mafia/love story, the OG fighting scenes (directed by Bruce himself) clearly have a soul that could never be recaptured upon Bruce’s untimely passing in 1973. No hipster-snob irony nor an unhealthy Bruce Lee obsession can defend this bad, but admirable, attempt at forcing a narrative onto exhibition (though the scene in which Billy is shot on set with a gun that’s supposed to have blanks was genuinely bone-rattling to watch, knowing his son Brandon’s fate years later).

Yet, it almost works. In the way that the first posthumous album from a dead rapper can still have glimpses of that spark of greatness, if you can stand a few forced collabos. 2Pac never would’ve rapped over that beat with that rapper, and Bruce Lee never would’ve gotten his ass kicked so many times trying to save a white girl (not on film, at least). But when someone’s gone that early on, we all can’t help but wish it didn’t all have to end so soon, even if we have to play pretend for a little bit.
