The Killing of a Chinese Bookie (John Cassavetes, 1976)

Posted on April 6, 2009
Filed Under Film Reviews |

The Killing of a a Chinese Bookie (1976) | dir./wr. John Cassavetes  | 139 min | USA

I’m not gonna front and act like I’ve seen more than one John Cassavetes film. But I’d bet neither have most of y’all so it’s all good. For me, that one film is The Killing of a Chinese Bookie (1976), which I only checked out awhile back out to see if it was as racist as the title made it sound.

Its about a badass strip club owner, Cosmo (Ben Gazzara - best known as “I’ve seen that guy in another movie but I can’t remember what”), who gets caught up with gambling debts to the mafia, who commands him to kill a Chinese guy or else be killed himself. Surface-level, it looks like your standard urban crime flick. Lesser directors equipped with the same story would find our dude in shoot-outs, chasing or being chased, occasionally fucking. Instead, Cassavetes builds the story around the shit we usually don’t see: long scenes chillin before and after hours, smoking cigarettes in sporadic, slow conversation, random phone conversations, fights that happen in the dark (in real time) like they do in real life, nowhere near convenient film lighting and quick-cut editing. If you’re looking or a compact, bookended plot, go elsewhere.

No, what’s remarkable about Killing is that it almost passes off as unremarkable–unless one is paying attention not just to how the story unfolds but also to how its told. It’s neither noir nor New Wave but borrows from both. One scene, lots of bare breasts, the next a whole lot of waiting. There’s no score unless you count the music actually being played within the scene. Looking back, it’s not surprising that Cassavetes’ name has come to be revered among other filmmakers and film geeks, especially those who champion the indie film or, at the very least, the word “indie.” There’s a rawness here not seen in all those other urban underbelly flicks. It’s the type of movie Martin Scorsese tries to make, and sometimes almost gets there, but never has and most likely never will. Because he integrates the criminal to the theater of cinema, and he does it well. Cassavetes brings cinema to the criminal. Or as close as possible for what’s still a staged, fictional story.
