Classic Duck Down Videos

Posted on July 28, 2009
Filed Under Music Videos |

Spent the previous month prepping the Blue Scholars website/blog relaunch to coincide with the announcement yesterday that we’ve inked a collaborative deal with Caffe Vita and Duck Down Records to put out our next two albums. I’ve been a Duck Down fan since Black Moon’s Who Got The Props? dropped in ‘93, and now I’m about to put out an EP and LP with their logo on the back? Crazy.

Revisiting mid-90s music videos from the Duck Down Records camp, I was reminded how much these cats have influenced the game not just with their music but also on the visual tip. Brooklyn has and continues to take it. If you don’t know, now you know.

Archived embedded media is not available for offline playback.

How Many MCs? - Black Moon (1994)

Any video with a rapper in an elevator will always remind me of this one, where a definite Brooklyn hip-hop music video aesthetic is clearly defined (also see: Jeru tha Damaja’s Come Clean and numerous Gang Starr videos). Project housing scenery interwoven with objects falling in the dark (bullets, water, glass), occasional jump-cuts (appropriated as the visual equivalent of a record scratch) and, of course, the classic tan Timberland boots. Lastly, Evil Dee goes in near the end!

Archived embedded media is not available for offline playback.



Leflaur Leflah Eshkoshka - OGC ft. Heltah Skeltah (1996)

On one hand, an exercise in simplicity at the dawn of the “JIggy” era. On the other hand, clowning the stylized studio-set white background look Hype Williams would try out if the budget didn’t meet his usual standards. Not to mention one of the classic songs of the entire ’90s. If you can’t recite at least a few bars from this one, you’re either young or you hate hip-hop or, worse, both.

Wrekonize (Remix) - Smif N Wessun (1995)

There’s something real Do The Right Thing-ish about this, and I’m not just talking bout the moving-background thing Spike does in his early films. I don’t know, maybe Spike did do this, considering he did work with Buckshot on the Crooklyn Dodgers video he directed. Even if he didn’t, then his influence is stamped all over this video, which is all good. Late 80s/early 90s Spike Lee is Brooklyn, and so is this track.
