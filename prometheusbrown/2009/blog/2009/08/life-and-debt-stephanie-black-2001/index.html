Life and Debt (Stephanie Black, 2001)

August 12, 2009



Life and Debt (2001) | dir. Stephanie Black | 80 min | USA

Opening: lush tropical greenery and seashore, interrupted by news broadcasts of the political and economic unrest and shots of the island’s residents staring blankly at the TV screen, which then cuts to an advertisement for Baskin-Robbins. Cut to Buju Banton walking through the shantytown reciting an acappella verse you don’t have to understand to feel. The bittersweet chants, stripped of its island rhythm and returned to its origins–the third world slum–forces the first world viewer to reconsider any romanticized notions of a tropical paradise. The documentary then moves into shots of tourists happily making their way through the airports, shuttles and hotels. It’s all voiced-over by Jamaica Kincaid, who addresses these tourists in second-person. This narration, adopted from her own book A Small Place, originally described her native Antigua from a native’s perspective.

The reasons behind why it so easily also describes Jamaica and many other “small places” becomes unraveled for the rest of the documentary. Tying the colonial past to the present, director Stephanie Black goes in on the IMF/World Bank and the WTO in an accessible manner. Instead of dwelling on numbers and isms, she gives concrete, visual (visceral) examples of imperialism’s (particularly the United States’s economic and military bullying foreign policy) effects on native workers. With interviews ranging from villagers and factory workers to former Jamaican Prime Minister, Michael Manley, the political slant comes off less as an angry treatise (see Michael Moore) or a generic plea for humanity (see everything else) and more as an objective, engaging hard look at real shit, with touches of humor and hope. Life and Debt is the story of one country, one island, that speaks for every small place.
