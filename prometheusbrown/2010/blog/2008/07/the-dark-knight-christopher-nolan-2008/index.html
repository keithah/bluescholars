The Dark Knight (Christopher Nolan, 2008)

July 20th, 2008 | 13 Comments »

The Dark Knight (2008)
dir. Christopher Nolan
150 min

Holy shitballs Batman! The Dark Knight arrives like a swift kick in the collective ass of all superhero flicks since, well – since Batman Begins (2004). 

Batman (Christian Bale) and Joker (Heath Ledger) face-off in an epic battle over Gotham City. Elevating that same-ol’ “good versus evil” scenario to a political level, TDK‘s lines are drawn as such: authority versus anarchy, idealism versus reality, justice versus justice. 

If Superman represents the might and righteousness of capitalism, Batman represents its darker half – the one that operates outside of its own laws to preserve itself (like the CIA, which, at one point, Magic Negro sidekick Lucius Fox (Morgan Freeman) compares to Wayne Industries). Batman’s vigilante war on crime weeded out the competition, and from this vaccuum, a worthy challenger emerges. While Bale’s Batman carried the weight of Batman Begins on his shoulders, TDK is all about the villains – first Joker, then the deterioration of Harvey Dent into Two-Face. But it’s still all Joker, all day.

As far as heroes and villains go, Batman and Joker match up perfectly. Both are individualists who, haunted by childhood trauma, are convinced that their opposing methods serve a necessary purpose for society. One sides with law and order, the other with complete subversion. Without one, the other wouldn’t exist, or at least would lack a justified existence. Standing apart from the many directors who simply adapt comic books into generic action flicks, Christopher Nolan (Memento, The Prestige) understands this hero/villain dynamic, delves deeply into it, but never loses sight of the action (unlike Ang Lee’s Hulk, 2005).

With all its chases, explosions and fights, TDK is at its best when Batman and Joker are trading not punches, but dialogue. Shit, any scene with Ledger’s haunting presence is a classic moment. The Joker’s entrance at the end of the opening bank robbery scene: classic. The disappearing pencil trick: extra classic. The stand-down with Batman speeding toward him: as good as any comic-book panel. 

I recall the initial disappointment among nerdboys everywhere when Heath Ledger was announced for Joker role. I always felt that while some actors are fit for comic-book roles based on looks alone (see Patrick Stewart as Professor X), others require true talent (see Robert Downey, Jr. as Iron Man). The Joker needed someone who could truly transform into the role. And Heath Ledger fucking killed it (and, perhaps, in the end, the role killed him – RIP). He channels the ultimate villain, rejecting the traditional over-theatrical psychopath. He’s serial-killer calm most of the time, with occasional, sudden bursts – even more scarier and believable. The choice of make-up and dark, instead of flashy, costume fit perfectly. 

Not shortchanging Ledger’s prodigious performance, but he merely fulfilled the task for any actor playing a villain. Just as Captain America’s archenemy in the 40s was a pseudo-Hitler, today’s most convincing Joker must project back to the audience a fear that already exists in their minds. As a character with oversimplified (political?) motivations, he becomes the ultimate post-9/11 villain – not motivated by anything real (or valid enough to be called a “cause”), only a twisted sense of justice and an inherent dislike for order and authority, almost as if he’s playing a game. It’s the current political climate that makes Ledger’s Joker the perfect villain at present, making Jack Nicholson’s incarnation (Batman, 1989) look like a mockery in comparison (ah, 1989…).

Batman and Joker’s ideals fit perfectly in the fantasyland of Gotham City, a slightly-flawed utopia, generally good with only handfuls of bad criminal elements. All police and public officials are upstanding (until they get half their face burnt) and all rich businesspeople are benevolent, unless they’re from Hong Kong. As much of a tour de force The Dark Knight is, I still gotta take issue with the underlying idealism it tries to sell. Gotham is a city visualized with the lens that reactionaries and neo-conservatives see our cities with; criminals are portrayed as abberations of a fair and rational society rather than the logical products of an unfair society. Batman, bad-ass that he is, probably loves Fox News.

Among superhero flicks, The Dark Knight towers above all, only rivaled by it’s predecessor Batman Begins and, perhaps, this year’s Iron Man. Because of Batman Begins, Hollywood learned that yes, it’s good to appease the rabid nerdboy fanbase (the OG fans deserve that much) and also bring in auteur-directors who will handle these revered characters with care and import their own unique vision into the story. Without spoiling anything, I’ll just say that Batman wins some, loses some and Joker’s story is left hanging (sequel? I can’t imagine anyone else filling Ledger’s void). Idealism wins again and Gotham’s status quo is preserved, which means Batman will still be employed (and going back into pre-production shortly).
