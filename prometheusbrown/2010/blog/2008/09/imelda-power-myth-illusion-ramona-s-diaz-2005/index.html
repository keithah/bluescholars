Imelda: Power, Myth, Illusion (Ramona S. Diaz, 2005)

Posted on September 21, 2008
Filed Under Film Reviews, Philippine Cinema |

Imelda: Power, Myth, Illusion (2005)
dir. Ramona S. Diaz
110 min

Imelda Marcos is known worldwide for one thing: “She’s never worn the same shoes twice.” There’s a cheap joke there about why so many Filipinos are sneakerheads which I’ll leave alone to say this: to many Filipinos, Imelda Marcos is a criminal, and we shall never forget it.

Imelda: Power, Myth, Illusion (2005), a retrospective of the former Philippine co-dictator’s life, finds her reminiscing on her early years as a provincial child and former almost-beauty-queen with lofty dreams and an affinity for “beauty,” narrowly defined by her twisted logic. At first it dwells, almost sentimentally, on her personal upbringing, then grows some teeth when documenting her ascendancy and rule as Ferdinand Marcos’ first lady, especially during the martial law era (1972-1986).

Filmmaker Ramona S. Diaz never fully reveals a particular political bias. She thoughtfully pads fascinating interview footage with Imelda (what a nutcase!) with talking-head commentary from all sides of the debate: apologetic family members, tortured journalists, former fearful associates, rival ruling-class politicians, critical priests.  

Recalling her role in the dictatorship, she believes she was “helping the poor” with her obsessive construction of first-world buildings to cover up the true state of Filipinos. A former journalist recalls that she had an “edifice complex.” 

Revealed through her circular logic and disconnect from reality (she even reads and draws diagrams from her self-help book Circle of Life), Imelda aspires for a cosmetic beauty. A colonized beauty that reveres Western art and architecture over indigenous culture and believes that God has mandated her every move. She talks like a politician, declaring that “I built this…” or “I did that…” for the people, admonishing her critics who were concerned with “superficial things like fixing houses” (her words, no lie).

As much as she is deserving of more indictments for plundering the Philippine people and the economy, Diaz’ approach allows Imelda to be honest and so open as to be self-indicting. The footage is subtly, almost cleverly, edited - one sequence of interviews recalls the collapse of one of her projects - a massive film center - followed by Imelda confiding, “since I was small, I only wanted to see beauty. If I see garbage, I turn my head away.” So she left the bodies there and commanded the workers to continue construction.

“She doesn’t face reality,” recalls a Jesuit priest who lived through martial law. Nearly everything coming from Imelda’s mouth backs up his statement. Justifying her extravagance, she says “I have to be a star for the poor people… and, become a slave so that everybody is a star.” What?

September 21 marks the anniversary of Proclamation 1081: Marcos’s 1972 declaration of martial law in the Philippines. Imelda only scratches the surface of that dark period of Philippine history, but reveals many connections between then and now. Today, exactly 36 years later, Marcos is dead and neither he nor Imelda have been held accountable for their crimes. To do so would mean that the current GMA regime, which has ruled Marcos-like over the Philippines since 2001, will have to one day be judged accordingly. 

Though her time in Philippine politics has passed, Imelda’s story reminds us that the Philippines that existed then remains now - only with different faces living lavishly in Malacanag and a whole lot of the cosmetic change Imelda would be proud of. Will we one day watch a documentary of Gloria Macapagal-Arroyo reminiscing gleefully about her murderous, exploitative regime? Let’s hope not.
