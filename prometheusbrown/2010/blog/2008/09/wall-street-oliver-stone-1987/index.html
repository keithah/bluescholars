Wall Street (Oliver Stone, 1987)

Posted on September 30, 2008
Filed Under Film Reviews |

Wall Street (1987)
dir. Oliver Stone
124 min

“We make the rules - the news, war, peace, famine, upheaval, the cost of a paper clip… you’re not naive enough to think we’re living in a democracy are you? It’s the free market.” - Gordon Gekko

Its recession time again (aka the cyclical crisis of capitalism). Market tumbling, banks crumbling, dollar sinking, unemployment rising and, now - the big debate over a US$700,000,000,000 taxpayer-paid corporate bailout. So Oliver Stone made a relevant, timely film shedding a critical light on this current economic shitstorm - which he wrote and directed more than 20 years ago.

Hopefully all the attention pouring over his upcoming George W. Bush biopic, W., spills over to Wall Street (1987), the closest thing to a critical look at the top layer of the “free” market that you’ll ever see from a big-studio American film. For the many of us who have no idea how the fuck the stock market works (though we all have that homie who pretends), the film reduces all the jargon and numbers into shit we can all understand: money, greed, more money, a little bit of sex, greed and more money.

With an ominous, dark New York City playing the background, Stone aims a direct shot at capitalism, with the investment banking sector as his bullseye. The greed that drives the market is personified in profit-hungry corporate raider Gordon Gekko (Michael Douglas), graduate of the Pat Riley fashion school and self-satisfied Republican asshole. He takes a younger version of himself, Bud Fox (Charlie Sheen), under his wing and grooms him to become a “player” in the ultimate game.

If a young Reaganomics could speak (without lying like its devout follwers do), it would talk just like Gordon Gekko. Douglas is fascinating to watch as the physical embodiment of neoliberal economics, most boldly played during his memorable speech at a shareholders meeting, plainly laying out his worldview without embellishment: “greed is good.” He has no time for lunch (it’s for wimps) or women (love is a myth) and will stop at no length, legal or otherwise, to make that next several million dollar transaction: using insider information, spying on competitors, bribing union leaders, reducing workers’ wages, etc.

Though Douglas won the Best Actor Oscar (1988) for this role, it’s Martin Sheen, as Bud Fox’s working-class father, who plays the film’s most pivotal role. In a world where money aint a thing (and is everything), he’s the concrete reality of the blue collar worker - a union leader who is keen on Douglas’ schemes to buy him out to increase company profits at the expense of his fellow workers. 

Without Martin Sheen, or with a lesser actor playing the role less convincingly, Wall Street would be another “businessman” movie devoid of their exploitative relationship to those who actually make shit. The elder Sheen/Esteves’ presence brings the reality of class inequality and, more importantly, struggle to the screen.

Bud, caught in the middle, is torn between loyalties. He doesn’t quite fit in anywhere- he’s the only suit in a blue collar Queens tavern and the cheapest suit in a bourgeois Manhattan restaurant. Each character representing an idea, Stone intuitively connects the dots between between bankers and brokers, investors and workers, business and business-owned media, governments and corporations.

“How much is enough?” Bud asks Gekko. He replies, “it’s not a question of enough, pal. It’s a zero sum game. Somebody wins, somebody loses.” In retrospect, Gekko wasn’t just a personification, but a prophecy. Gekko survived beyond the closing credits of Wall Street and went on to see the fuzing of investment banking with commercial banking in the late 1980s, the free-market expansion of the 1990s and the real-estate speculation craze of the first half of the 2000s. Then it all came crashing down. 

They say the 80s is back - they’re not lying. Beyond the cocaine, neon/pastel colors, tight jeans, high-tops, the Lakers vs. Celtics - the decade’s biggest legacy - Reaganomics - has brought us where we are today. Oliver Stone is hit and miss (lately more the latter than the former), but he hits the target with this one, exposing who the real players are. Twenty years down the spiral since Wall Street, with the bubble having finally burst, banks crashing and stock market reeling, Gordon Gekko is on life support. And Washington is saying it’s gonna take $700 Billion to keep the machine on.
