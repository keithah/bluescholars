Synecdoche, New York (Charlie Kaufman, 2008)

December 13th, 2008 | 2 Comments »

Synecdoche, New York (2008) | dir. & wr. Charlie Kaufman | 124 min | USA

This here is a movie about a playwright making a play about a playwright making a play based on his own life, which is based, loosely, on the life of the movie’s writer who, of course, is, and can only be, Charlie Kaufman. And, believe me, that’s an oversimplification of this meandering mindfuck of a film that plunges us through the depths of existentialism with nary a revelation to reward its laborious narrative.

With Kaufman making the leap from writer to first-time director, you’d think that the weirdo shit that made his previous screenplays so successful would make Synecdoche something magical. Instead of the surreal absurdity of Being John Malkovich, the humorous self-reflexivity of Adaptation or the charm of Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind, we get straight-up depression. So who better to cast than Philip Seymour Hoffman? He plays Caden Cotard, a tortured health-ailing theater director who, convinced that death is around the corner, instead sees his production grow larger (he recreates New York City inside a warehouse) and longer (40 years pass by) to a point beyond grandeur or even psychosis, which, at one point, he claims not to have (um, right).

He’s tortured by the mostly-failed relationships with the women in his life: his ex-wife Adele (Catherine Keener) and their daughter Olivia. His therapist with a boil on her foot whose advances he’s oblivious to. The box-office girl Hazel (Samantha Morton), whose house is always on fire, who he falls in love with. His emotionally needy lead actress Claire (Michelle Williams), who plays Hazel in the play. The girl who replaces Claire to play another Hazel. And the lady (Diane Wiest) who cleans Adele’s apartment. And it goes on. No, really. It goes on and on and on...

For half the film, I was entranced, wondering how it would end. For the other half, I just wanted it to end. Strangely, when it finally happened, I wanted to write. Something, anything. Actually, everything. I can’t say that I was entertained by Synecdoche, but I can say that it induced me to Thizz Face several times and ponder why I make art and who I do it for. Probably not a choice film to watch just moments before stepping onstage to rap in front of a sold-out crowd (Hawthorne Theater in Portland), nor on a cold, rainy Northwest December afternoon. But maybe a year or so from now, I’ll probably watch it again, enjoy it and “get it” alot more, just as I had to do with all the other Charlie Kaufman joints.
