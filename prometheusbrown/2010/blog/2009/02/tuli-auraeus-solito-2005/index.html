Tuli (Auraeus Solito, 2005)

Posted on February 15, 2009
Filed Under Film Reviews, Philippine Cinema |

Tuli (2005) | dir. Auraeus Solito, wr. Jimmy Flores | 113 min | Philippines (Tagalog w/ English subtitles)

Tuli is the most original movie about circumcision, bisexuality and religion I’ve ever seen. OK, it’s the only movie I’ve ever seen that fits that description, which makes it worthy of attention alone (if you know of any others let me know). But the way these themes are layered in a distinctly Filipino manner — via an unapologetic embrace of digital video — make it a unique viewing experience. Chances are, you’ve never seen a film like this and, when you finally do, you probably never will again.

From the same dude who crafted the brilliant Ang Pagdadalaga ni Maximo Oliveros (director Auraeus Solito), Tuli is ten times darker (literally and figuratively - over half the film occurs either at night or in tree shade), more graphically sexual and violent, and wields a sharper political sword than its predecessor. With echoes of Lino Brocka and Kidlat Tahimik’s countryside-set films mixed with folkloric mysticism (Solito cites Tahimik’s Turumba as a big influence), Tuli is a thematic and technical departure from Ang Pagdadalaga, which sported more hand-held verite and barrio cacophany in an urban setting. Tuli is set in the countryside, quiet for long stretches of time, and meticulously photographed like a painting. 

Two stories run through Tuli. One is centered around a rural Filipino family: the young, innocent but headstrong Daisy (Desiree Del Valle), her submissive mother (Ama Quiambao), and Ka Malteng, the family’s patriarch — the village mangtutuli, or official circumciser (Bembol Roco strikes again) — a ruthless drunk who beats wife and daughter when he’s not slicing foreskins. As his physical and mental health wanes, Daisy and her best friend Botchok (Vanna Garcia) begin exploring their attraction toward each other. 

The other story is about a tight-knit countryside Third World community. More specifically, contradictions in feudal Philippine village culture: its Catholic Church-influenced traditions and indigenous folklore, conservative sexual mores, filial duty, proclivity towards tsismis, guilt etc. The attention paid to traditions both indigenous and colonial (self-flaggellation processions, serenading, a babaylan) add a historic dimension to the modern story.

With the exception of Daisy and Botchok, however, most other characters lumber along perfunctorily - either an unintended shortcoming in Jimmy Flores‘ screenplay or an intended statement that feudalism forces people into rigid, unchanging identities. Unlike the ambiguity of gender roles in Ang Pagdadalaga, the lines in Tuli are drawn more clearly: men are patriarchal dictators while women yield an almost mystical power. This is true for everyone except for Nanding (Carlo Aquino), the lone uncircumcised man in the village who unwittingly steps into a sexually complicated love triangle with the two ladies.

Technically, Tuli is a masterpiece by its budget’s and resources’ standards, but even some limitations can not be overcome by astute filmmaking. The photographic warmth of film is lost in Solito’s digital translation, but he makes the most of it with creative blocking (mosquito nets, trees, walls with small windows) and use of natural lighting. Most scenes are worthy of being called picturesque, but a few are public access-esque. And the sound, oh the sound. Dialogue between characters in long shots are barely audible and some scenes are overly scored (and overly loud at times). But for the film’s story and approach, these flaws are minor - even welcome - considering Solito’s DIY approach and filmmaking philosophy. 

Though it played at the 2007 Sundance and Berlinale Film Festivals and won the grand jury prize for International narrative feature at Outfest (an LGBT film festival in LA), Tuli failed to match the hype of its predecessor. Perhaps it was too sinister, too sexually daring for those who adored Ang Pagdadalaga’s colorful melodrama and innocent love. The Philippines’ MTRCB thought so, stamping it with an X-rating for its one girl/girl kissing scene and roadblocking it from a wide local release. Which is too bad, but also a fitting example of the sort of hypocrisy toward sexuality that Solito, himself an openly queer man, takes a cut at.
