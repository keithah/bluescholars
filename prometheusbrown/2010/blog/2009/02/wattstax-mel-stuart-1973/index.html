Wattstax (Mel Stuart, 1973)

Posted on February 13, 2009
Filed Under Film Reviews |

Wattstax (1973) | dir. Mel Stuart | 98 min | USA

“All of us have something to say, but some are never heard. Over seven years ago the people of Watts stood together and demanded to be heard. On a Sunday this past August in Los Angeles Coliseum, over 100,000 Black people came together to commemorate that moment in American history. For over six hours.. the living word in a soulful expression of the Black experience. This a film about that experience, and what the people have to say.” 

… says Richard Pryor as he introduces Wattstax (1973). Though he’s prominently featured throughout, he’s but a minor part of what makes this an essential American documentary. Filmed in 1972 around a Stax Records-promoted concert referred to by some as the Black Woodstock (fuck that, Woodstock was the White Wattstax), it appears to be another concert film. The lineup of artists — most of whom I was introduced to decades later via hip-hop — was ridiculous: The Emotions, Rufus Thomas, Bar-Kays, Johnnie Taylor, Albert King, Isaac Hayes, to name a few.

A collection of performances alone would’ve made a decent, if unremarkable film. At least for folks (like this bonehead New York Times critic) who care more about the music and less about the people who make it. But what makes Wattstax remarkable is the stuff between the performances (except perhaps Isaac Hayes’ dramatic outfit unveiling!). Real talk scenes featuring everyday folks from workers to musicians, and interviews with Richard Pryor, Ted Lange and others jump from one hot topic to the next: God, love, racism, adultery, politics. The back-and-forth movement between performance and conversation footage reveals that the music is married to its social conditions: what was going on in Black community, and in America, at the time (much mention of Black Panther influences, the Vietnam War, the “Civil Rights” struggle). The chronological order of events, the synchronization of conversations and performances, images and music - is textbook documentary filmmaking - simple, structured, and no wasted footage. I’d bet that an equally dope documentary could be made with the cutting room scraps.

More than just a glimpse of the fashion and music, today’s viewer can’t help but feel the dissonance between Black media representation today compared to how openly and casually militant Black media representation was a generation ago. In part, the media reinforced this image (the more threatening they seem, the more justified the repression), but the reality was that a people’s struggle was reaching an organizational peak and a cultural renaissance. Other nationalist movements followed suit - Native, Asian, Latino, cool white people - all, inspired by the Black struggle, and fearless in saying what’s on their mind. Most of what folks say in Wattstax isn’t unlike anything the Rev. Jeremiah Wright was crucified by the media for last year. Is this expression of a militant identity some “old” shit - necessary then, useless now? It’s hard to imagine seeing something in movies or TV in 2009 like Wattstax’s invocation featuring Rev. Jesse Jackson (rocking a dashiki) performing his “I Am Somebody” poem. 

So to all my history teachers out there - since your school’s curriculum probably encourages you to embrace diversity and celebrate Black History Month - do your students a favor and play this film for them. It’ll be a good look for everyone. I learned more about race and America in the 1970s from a concert documentary than any of those old boring PBS documentaries we used to sleep through.
