Entre Les Murs/The Class (Laurent Cantet, 2008)

Posted on March 3, 2009
Filed Under Film Reviews, French Cinema |

Entre Les Murs/The Class (2008) | dir. Laurent Cantet, wr. François Bégaudeau | 124 min | France (French w/ English subtitles)

A well-meaning white teacher in a class full of brown and black, mostly immigrant, students struggles to keep them attentive and disciplined. The black kids sit in the back, listen to rap music, and are confrontational. Two Asian computer-nerds sit up front, don’t speak much, but get the best grades. There’s one goth outcast who gets picked on by everyone. The rest of the teachers in the school seem to have given up on these kids, but our protagonist sees potential in them and many flaws in the school’s disciplinary policies. He does his best to keep the tension bottled until it explodes one day in his face.

How many movies like this do we really need? Based on that description alone, you’d have to drag me unconscious to the theater to get me to watch it. But my interest was piqued when I started hearing about this simple little film that won the Palme d’Or at 2008 Cannes, recieved an Academy Award nomination (for Best Foreign FIlm), and finally got picked up for short-run screenings this past month. I missed the Seattle run, but happened to in San Francisco this past week where it’s playing at the Clay Theater.

To my surprise, Entre Les Murs (The Class) is a perfect example of how an excellent film can be derived from an ordinary, even cliched, narrative if the filmmaker(s) know what they’re doing. French director Laurent Cantet does this via documentary-style storytelling and throwing out that annoying redemption arc that plagues the genre. He partners with writer François Bégaudeau, a teacher and novelist who plays a version of himself (Mr. Francois Marin) in the film’s lead role, based on his own novel and first-hand experiences. It’s all given that minimal French realist “slice-of-life” approach: nuanced and naturally flowing with interruptions, awkward moments, silences etc. in a series of snapshots over the span of a school year set completely within school walls. No dramatized visits to the “hood,” no teachers crying in frustration at home. No musical score. Everywhere, contradiction: teachers arguing with one another over how to discipline students, racial tensions, working conditions, power dynamics, etc. Francois  constantly spars with his students — the real stars of the film — in scenes that, despite being set in a country thousands of miles away, resembled my own classrooms far better than any American movie about the public school experience. The topic of national identity (the students’ lower-class backgrounds is already a given) is apparently as big an issue there as it is here, perhaps moreso.

Most classroom films are simply about teachers and students, overcoming the odds, with the school institution as the backdrop, wrapped in spectacle and convenient resolutions. Entre Les Murs flips the formula. It’s about the institution, but told through one class. It’s a story about odds that, no matter how hard you try, how sincere your motives, are never overcome. The liberal’s fantasy of “saving” underpriviliged youth and receiving their gratitude in return is smashed to bits in the film’s final scene. So depressingly simple yet gut-checking, it’s a statement so potent precisely because it almost makes none at all. We usually see the teacher, weighing effectiveness against weariness, constantly asking him/herself, “why?”. Which is then conveniently followed by some reassurance of their missionary purpose. Here, it’s the students who ask “why?” and, given no reassurances, either fail or move on.
