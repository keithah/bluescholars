District 9 (Neill Blomkamp, 2009)

August 15, 2009



District 9 (2009) | dir. Neill Blomkamp  | 112 min | South Africa/New Zealand

Nothing unites the human race like an alien invasion.

That’s lesson number one for any 80s baby who grew up either watching them threaten our existence (Alien, V-the TV mini-series) or play misunderstood, benevolent beings who can teach us how to truly be human (Close Encounters of the Third Kind, E.T., Enemy Mine). Alas, the alien’s place in pop culture seemed to have its victory lap in the 90s with Independence Day and The X-Files and rappers talking ’bout Elohim and such. After the fall of the Soviet Union, what was once a thinly-veiled Cold War metaphor no longer captivated our hearts and minds (and fears). Subsequently, more recent Alien Invasion films like War of the Worlds, Signs and, especially, the remake of The Day The Earth Stood Still, all ate shit. 

But, what if, instead of coming to Earth as hostiles, they came as refugees of a wartorn planet and we became their oppressor? Not a new idea, either (see Alien Nation). So what makes District 9 (2009) distinct from its lineage? Perhaps nothing much, but it still blows away any of the over-exploding big-studio garbage coming from Hollywood these days. Plus its filmed better, doesn’t have an annoying score, darkly humorous, and most of all, devoid of quasi-pro-military shit.

The plot: a defunct mothership hangs over Johannesburg, where 1.5 million alien refugees landed 20 years ago, got hated on, and then got herded into District 9. We follow a bumbling weapons manufacturing company officer (Sharlto Copley) in charge of leading a mass eviction of District 9 (think Michael Scott in The Office, with blood and explosions). He gets infected, and slowly begins to transform into a human-alien hybrid, which makes him an object of desire by everyone from a multinational weapons manufacturer interested in alien technology to a local Nigerian warlord who wants to eat his arm to gain spiritual power. Along the way, he learns tolernace for his fellow sentient beings.

Sci-Fi has always been a safe haven for social commentary. And it’s best done when subtle instead of screaming. District 9’s commentary screams–literally–with intergalactic race riots, repetition of derogatory terms (”prawns”), weapons profiteering. Basically, humans of all colors acting towards the aliens the way Israel does to Palestinians. Or white Afrikaaners to South Africans–director Neill Blomkamp based the film’s premise on his childhood in South Africa during apartheid. A worthwhile, if guilt-ridden, statement on race and intolerance (a Hollywood tradition since D.W Griffith’s Intolerance, 1919) gets muddled in the films depictions of its black African characters, which I’ll leave alone since it’s been so eloquently questioned here.

So, the old myth about aliens uniting humans holds true in District 9’s post-racial society. It also suggests the darker side of that unity, which few other sci-fi films dared touch: nothing unites the human race more than its own inhumanity.
