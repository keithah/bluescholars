Mandabi (Ousmane Sembene, 1968)

September 10, 2009

Mandabi/The Money Order (1968) | dir. Ousmane Sembene | 90 min | Senegal (Wolof w/ English Subtitles)

If you’re from an immigrant community, you’re probably familiar with the process of sending money “back home.” It isn’t so much a noble gesture as it is a bittersweet necessity, and, no matter how normalized this story has become in a “global” society, it’s an endless source of tragic humor. One that the late Senegalese writer-director Ousmane Sembene went in on with Mandabi (1968), a film no less charming than it is a scathing tract against colonialism, capitalism and, most of all, the complacency of those who suffer most from it.

Ibrahim, a middle-aged patriarch in Dakar, Senegal, receives a money order from his nephew, a street sweeper in Paris. After living it up for a day, he suddenly realizes the bureacratic nightmare of getting the order cashed: he needs an ID, which requires an expensive photograph and a non-existent birth certificate. Meanwhile, bored, corrupt clerks give him the run-around and his family starts bugging. Then it gets all bad: one of his three wives has told a neighbor, and suddenly everybody and their moms (literally) are at his door trying to get a piece. A litany of problems compounded by his illiteracy and inability to say no.

Serious business, but wrapped in a comedy of absurdity that tells stories even when there’s no dialogue. Like this powerfully subtle scene (pictured above), reminiscent of one in Charles Burnett’s Killer of Sheep (1977) years later, of a young black girl playing with a white doll. Sembene’s talents as a writer (a celebrated novelist before he became a filmmaker at 40) shines in the intricately woven layers of this simple story, the filming far beyond anything you’d expect from a third world filmmaker making only his second film. The ending is neither a cop-out nor a convenient bookend, but something so much more in its simplicity and questioning. Simply put, Mandabi is a masterpiece. If you’re not familiar with Ousmane Sembene, get familiar. Mandabi would be a good place to start.
