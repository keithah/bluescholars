35 Ruhms/35 Shots of Rum (Claire Denis, 2009)

January 11th, 2010 | 2 Comments »

35 Shots of Rum (2009) opens with Lionel (Alex Descas), a middle-aged commuter train driver, and his daughter, Josephine (Mati Diop), making their way home for dinner. On the way back to their modest housing project apartment in suburban Paris, Josephine buys a new rice cooker. Lionel arrives home as Josephine, who is cooking, greets him lovingly, comments that he smells like cigarette smoke, and hands him his house slippers. He pulls out a gift: a new rice cooker, which she adores. She doesn’t tell him she already bought one. The silences between them are loud. In the next scene, Josephine is in a college classroom. The entire class, all-Black, including the professor, are discussing Franz Fanon and the political and psychological effects of colonization. Josephine, whose father is Black and deceased mother is white, replies to a classmate, ” debt can be discussed without getting full of emotion.” The politics surrounding her existence suddenly becomes quiet.

But it’s still there. As it is in every film directed by Claire Denis, whose favorite subjects are the people her native country has colonized (she grew up in French colonial Africa). Since Chocolat (1988), she’s made a name for herself by taking the meditative, lyrical style of classic (bourgeois) French cinema and marrying it to the lesser-seen people of France. Except for a few cobblestone streets, Denis makes France look like industrial anywhere. Much of 35 Shots happens either at night, inside small apartments or cafes, or on subway tracks. Her characters speak sparingly–nearly half of 35 Shots of Rum happens without dialogue. All Lionel and Josephine have are each other, struggling even to connect emotionally with their long-time neighbors, whom Josephine considers “family”: Gabrielle, a taxi driver who’s crushing on Lionel, and Noe, the slick, well-dressed neighbor Josephine can’t seem to express her love for. It’s not as serious as it all sounds, though: a glance between Lionel and Gabrielle over the balcony ends with Lionel easing into the couch and farting. Noe deadpanningly disposes of his 17-year old dead cat in the trash can, Josephine expresses disgust.

There is no climax hinted at for our four protagonists (the end comes suddenly). No great tragedies (somebody does die, though). The emotional bursts we’ve come to expect from repressed movie characters never come (except once, briefly, for Josephine). But somehow, they have all been transformed by film’s end: four strangers, four “outsiders” of urban France, finding each other, trying to find words for relationships that have developed for the most part without them.
