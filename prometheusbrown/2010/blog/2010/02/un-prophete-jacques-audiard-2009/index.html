Un prophète (Jacques Audiard, 2009)

February 13th, 2010 | 3 Comments »

It’s been awhile since I was blown away by a gangster flick. Public Enemies came close but left me wanting, and a few recent foreign films (Tsotsi, Gomorrah) were worthy but overhyped. There was a time when the homies and I would watch Blood In, Blood Out and Menace II Society ad nauseam. Hip-hops mid-90s mafia infatuation led us to Scarface and The Godfather (which is permanently fixed in my all-time top five). Watching a barrage of gangster flicks in the last decade (pretty much everything after Don’t Be A Menace) play like a parody of previous ones, I often wondered if the genre had grown stale or if I had outgrown my adolescent hunger for onscreen violence. Watching Un prophète confirmed that there’s is life yet for the epic gangster film.

Like Blood In Blood Out, Un prophète follows the trail of a young man who enters prison and is forced to choose among warring ethnic groups for “protection,” and finding it within an organized crime network. Nineteen-year-old Malik El Djebena (Tahar Rahim) is an illiterate Arab who, after being roughed up by his own people in a Corsican prison, gets recruited by Corsican mafia don César Luciani (Niels Arestrup) to become first an assassin, then an assistant. On one hand, they protect him from other prisoners, but on the other hand they berate him, hurl racial slurs at him and beat him up to remind him who he works for. Malik’s first killing is one of the most starkly filmed scenes in recent memory, violent even by gangster flick standards. Things change when Malik begins taking reading classes under a fellow Arab who opens the door into a whole ‘nother underworld. When Malik finally earns work release furloughs a year later, he finds himself in the middle of the delicate on-and-off war between the Arab and Corsican mafias.

There’s nothing cinematically groundbreaking here, though it’s shot really well in a style that echoes many prison-based films. Overhead shots of “the yard,” long shots from one end of the yard to the antagonists on the other end. And the story, about a young man entering prison as a petty thug and emerging from it a kingpin, is nothing special. The acting isn’t spectacular, but convincing. Part of Un prophète’s allure is that we’re watching Arabs and Corsicans, two ethnicities we don’t usually see represented in cinematic gangland. But something else makes Un prophète work: it’s simplicity. It’s as if it were an homage to the entire gangster flick genre itself, rather than an emulation gone parody.
