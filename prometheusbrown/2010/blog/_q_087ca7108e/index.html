Metropolis (Fritz Lang, 1927)

Posted on July 20, 2008
Filed Under Films |

Metropolis (1927)
dir. Fritz Lang
117 min

Metropolis (1927) is the perfect expression of petty-bourgeois class ideology: sympathetic towards the plight of poor, dirty workers but trusting and in awe of their benevolent, if aloof, rulers. As much as it is revered as a landmark moment in cinema artistry and form, I believe it is the film’s political content - director Fritz Lang’s appeal to both oppressor and oppressed - that resonates today, in large part a result of the enduring myth that the class struggle can be mediated (it can’t).

When I first watched (about half of) Metropolis ten years ago (I was 18 or 19), I didn’t get it. I doubt that many people educated in a US public high school and exposed to American popular entertainment would at first. For a child of the 80’s, it was a chore to watch - there was no dialogue, the soft-focused black and white stationary shots and superimpositions looked old as shit, and, most of all, it actually required - no, demanded - me to think. Although I never finished the film (until yesterday), I remember the feeling of being challenged by a film rather than being entertained or spoonfed. It changed the way I watched film, permanently (So much that I don’t even call them “movies” anymore, just film!).

It’s interesting to see that as far back as the 1920’s, visionary artists had already been making bleak forecasts for the 21st century. Released in 1927 during the stable years of Germany’s Weimar Republic before Hitler started stomping upon the left, Lang’s spectacular tour de fence has 1920’s written all over it. The future is made in the decade’s image - an era of rapid industrialization: steel, concrete and steam, where machines, once at our control, now seem to command us.

Aesthetically, scenes and shots are as breathtaking as later films that had access to superior technology. For a silent film steeped in politics and philosophy, Metropolis is astonishingly short on text. Instead, facial expressions and theatrics (and what must have been the dopest special effects back then) drive the story forward.

The most revealing evidence of Lang’s petty-bourgeois schizophrenia is that the city of Metropolis has no middle class (gasp!); only workers (the “hands”) and administrators (the “head”). Lang is sympathetic and critical towards both. The hands live underground, work 10 hour shifts, and are quickly replaced when any of them die. But they’re also unruly, simple and predisposed to unnecessary violence. The heads, of course, live in decadent affluence and ignorance. But they’re so care-free, pretty and sophisticated! Stable as Metropolis may be, it seems only a matter of time until the workers turn on their employers.

Freder, son of Fredersen, Lord of Metropolis, and a 1920’s Leonardo diCaprio clone, goes slumming and is shocked like Paris Hilton walking through a Paris ghetto. Perhaps out of upper-class guilt or being lovestruck by a lower-class woman, or both, he pleads with his father to have mercy on the workers, gets rejected, and joins the masses. The woman of his affections is Maria, a hottie/prophet who preaches civil disobedience to the workers, using biblical references (such as the Tower of Babel) to give them hope. All this hope is channeled into awaiting a “mediator” who will bring about a new peace between the two classes. Thus, Metropolis is also perhaps the first (or most notable) of a long tradition of messianic Jesus-allegory films (Jon/Maria = Jesus/Mary).

Freder’s father conspires with the mad scientist Rotswag to create a robot-clone of Maria. The fake Maria does a strip tease (Mary Magdalene?), then is instructed to incite the workers into rebellion so that Lord Fredersen can justify a swift, violent repression and quell any real challenge to his power. If one was left wondering through the first 3/4 of Metropolis what Lang thinks of revolution, the climax reveals it explicitly. Without their realizing it, the workers’ destruction of the machines leads to the flooding of their city - a condemnation of armed revolution (which is treated as terrorism - also to become a longstanding mainstream cinematic tradition).

Right before Metropolis is about to eat shit and die, Freder and the real Maria come to the rescue. The workers have suffered enough in their backfiring rebellion, and it seems Lord Fredersen, through his son’s compassion and love for Maria, is learning to treat his underground people with more respect. In the final scene, Maria says “there can be no understanding between the hands and the head unless the heart acts as the mediator.” Freder then, literally, joins the hands of the workers’ Foreman, and his fathers’, reminiscent of a scene from a Bob Marley documentary where he’s uniting the hands of political opponents at a concert. A powerful temporary statement, yes, but one that ducks the real question of who shall rule: the people or the plunderers?

In a way, Bob Marley and Fritz Lang had similar visions - critical of the rich, love for the poor, and believing that art/love can overcome a little trouble. Such universality is what makes art made with this purpose timeless and palatable, but, at the same time, a little toothless. In the world of silent film, if Birth of a Nation is racist-ass Woodrow Wilson and Battleship Potemkin is revolutionary Lenin, then Metropolis is that one Buddhist monk who set himself on fire. An inspiring, spectacular moment that, when, once the flames die out, we’re left asking, now what?
