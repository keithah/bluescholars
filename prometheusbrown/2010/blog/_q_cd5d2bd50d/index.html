City After Dark (Manila By night) (Ishmael Bernal, 1980)

Posted on July 22, 2008
Filed Under Films |

Over the next few days, leading up to president Gloria Macapagal-Arroyo’s annual State of the Nation Address (SONA) and in solidarity with those standing up to protest, I will be reviewing Philippine films in line with BAYAN-USA’s efforts to address the real state of Filipinos worldwide.

—–

City After Dark (Manila By Night) (1980)
dir. Ishmael Bernal
150 min
Philippines

Before the slow stroll down assimilation road had moms replacing sinigang and pinakbet with spaghetti and canned food, when pops still had the nerve to slaughter a stinking goat in front of the haole neighbors, they, along with my aunties and uncles, when not gambling, would occasionally watch Beta tapes of Philippine movies. Wanting instead to watch cartoons or some shit on our only TV, I remember hating those nights. But I watched many of these films anyway, forced to “cober” my eyes whenever breasts appeared on the screen. I can’t remember if Ishmael Bernal’s City After Dark/Manila By Night (1983) was one of those movies, but after just recently viewing a DVD copy I randomly stumbled upon at a pawnshop, memories of those days in mid-1980s Hawaii started flooding back.

City After Dark is late, prolific Philippine director Ishmael Bernal’s (1938-1996) - and, arguably, according to a quote on the DVD sleeve, Philippine cinema’s - masterpiece. Set in nighttime Manila and featuring an ensemble cast of young, fair-skinned city-dwellers, the plot jumps around from one character to the next, slowly connecting the dots that bind them: love, lust, drugs, prostitution, rebellion against family and tradition, and, most of all, search for hope in a hot, despearate third-world metropolis suffocating under dictator Ferdinand Marcos’ martial law rule.

But Marcos and his opposition are absent in the story; their existence and conflict are only implied by the city’s ugliness, opposite of Marcos optimistic proclamations: ruthless police, unemployed workers turning to thievery and prostitution. Released the same year I was born (1980), it occurred to me that I was watching a time capsule of the very place my parents left when they were around the same age as these characters. And it was easy to see why.

Manila is a scarred beauty whose children scream to be heard. Bernal paints it with the same brush that 1940-50s Italian neorealist directors did post-war Rome (De Sica, Antonioni), with characters clutching a humanity the city wants to snuff out. It’s one of those rare movies that makes you question if it really is just a movie, or perhaps something more. Without containing any overtly political dialogue or themes, the film itself is a political statement.

After being numbed by many films either too mediocre or too distant from my reality, City After Dark lit a powderkeg in my chest. It reminded me that, yes!, there is a rich tradition of quality Philippine cinema that I (and most of my US-raised Pin@y peers) have ignored as we throw our hard-earned dollars at Hollywood garbage that features not one single actor that looks like us, not one single word that speaks our native tongue, not one story that tells us where we’ve been and where we are. So, no wonder we don’t know where were going (or can go).

I realize that being initially kept from our history is a product of colonial amnesia, among many factors we have little control over. But for those of us who, in our earnest attempts to decolonize, have gained access to doors previously hidden to us, there are no more excuses to not unlock them. In many ways, Manila and the rest of the country, despite the appearance of megamalls and US-inspired vapid entertainment, suffer the same contradictions (if not worse) as those illuminated by City After Dark (and many other Philippine films, especially by dir Lino Brocka). Thus, for young Filipinos removed from their homeland, City After Dark must become more than just “our parents’ movie.” More than almost anything else we see on a flickering screen, this is our story, too.
