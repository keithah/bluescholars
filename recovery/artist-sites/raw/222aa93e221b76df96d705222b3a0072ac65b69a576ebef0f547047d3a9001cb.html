(500) Days of Summer (Marc Webb, 2009)

August 22, 2009



(500) Days of Summer | dir. Marc Webb | 95 min | USA

Despite looking and feeling like just another romantic comedy by, about and for middle-class white people, (500) Days of Summer goes out of its way to tell you that it isn”t. The baritone narrator even tells you himself “this isn’t a love story.” And it does it well, both honoring and bucking the cliches of its genre with a refreshing take on an old formula (A male version of He’s Not That Into You). The romantic myth isn’t so much rejected as it is (post-)modernized, its observations not so much new as painfully obvious. Tom (Joseph Gordon-Levitt), a once-aspiring architect turned greeting-card writer, meets new co-worker Summer (Zooey Deschanel). She isn’t looking to settle but he thinks she’s the one, and in the meantime they become fuck buddies.

Familiar recipe for eventual meltdown. But for all the p-whipped feelings-catching men and the detached, non-commitant women we all know in real life, how often do you see that dynamic play out onscreen? (500) Days does well in mirroring the best and worst of these partner traits back to an audience ready to see something different (i.e. themselves). Tom and Summer have a fractured, sometimes awkward, chemistry that fits their relationship well. For both actors, these were their first roles that didn’t annoy the shit out of me.

While I dug the non-linear flashback/flashforward story structure, my biggest critique is the quick-editing and and gimmicky scenes. Lots of meta-narration, split-screens, and fourth-wall-breaking going on here. It even turns into a mock-musical for a few minutes. Makes perfect sense considering first-time director Marc Webb’s vast experience making music videos (Fergie, Diddy, Nelly to name a few). Stylistically, (500) Days is perfectly crafted for the MTV generation, but with a subtle cinematic awareness and inside references film geeks will appreciate (or find gratuitous).

One particular scene transcends its surface novelty: a real-time split screen scene with Tom’s “expectations” on the left and “reality” on the right. It’s been done before, but rarely this well. It is the film’s defining scene as well as its boldest statement on relationships itself. A rejection of the traditional idealism Hollywood (and greeting cards and American culture in general) has been selling us, and a small step towards a playful realism today’s ADD-ridden 80s babies crave. A dialectical declaration on love–that in our relationships there is always something born and growing, and something decaying and ceasing to be.
