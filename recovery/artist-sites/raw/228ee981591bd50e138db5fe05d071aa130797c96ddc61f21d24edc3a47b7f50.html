SIFF 2010 Review: Tehroun (Nader T. Homayoun, 2009)

June 8th, 2010 | 1 Comment »

“This must be the raciest Iranian film ever,” the homie Zia (who’s Iranian) remarked fifteen minutes into Tehroun (Nader T. Homayoun, 2009). Funny because it wasn’t racy at all, and yet, that statement is probably true. At least from the handful of Iranian films that usually get film festival looks. You’d think, from watching these films, that 1. 90% of the Iranian population is male and 2. everybody’s sad. Delving further into the urban underworld that Crimson Gold (Jafar Panahi, 2006) surface-scratched, Tehroun plays like the most serious comedy ever. You almost need to have watched several of Abbas Kiarostami’s (Taste of Cherry) stonefaced meditation-flicks to catch the humor in this one. Or, do what I did and watch it with a couple of Iranians who will tell you so.

The first half is Three Iranian Men and a Baby. But the baby is a rental–a prop used for increased begging success. The three dudes, living in a cramped slum hotel room, fumble through diaper changes and argue over who’s responsible for the crying babe. The baby-renter is Ibrahim, who is expecting a child himself. Another man looks like a light-skinned Spike Lee. He is the comic relief, but more in a sad-buffoon than court-jester kind of way. The third man, younger brother of the baby-renter, is referred to many times as a young virgin, supposedly barely an adult though he looks at least 30. When his brother asks him to watch the baby for one afternoon, the most clothed prostitute ever solicits him, and dashes away with the child while he finds a hotel room. The second half, with the baby gone before we can give a shit about it, gets dead serious when the gangsters show up to collect on their lost goods. Nothing good can come out of this, and nothing good does.

Tehroun left me with the same essential feeling I get from other Iranian films I’ve dug. A combination of “that was dope” and “that’s it?” Simple story about poor folk caught in the hustle, glimpses of city life, subtle political statements (like the Spike Lee clone mocking the Imam’s marriage prayer under his breath). What’ll make Tehroun memorable for me, however, are the two women who played prominently in forcing the films plot: the prostitute, who had to somehow convey that she was a woman of the night using only her face and voice, and Ibrahim’s wife, who decided to take matters in her own hands in light of her husband’s failures.

Tehroun screens at Uptown Cinemas tonight (Tue, June at 7 P.M.
