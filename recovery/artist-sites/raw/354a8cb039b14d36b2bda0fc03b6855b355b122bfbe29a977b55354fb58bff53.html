Inglourious Basterds (Quentin Tarantino, 2009)

August 28, 2009

Inglourious Basterds (2009) | dir. Quentin Tarantino | 154 min | USA

Quentin Tarantino’s back again, doing what he does best: a revenge flick embedded with cinema references, long conversations and drenched in blood. Inglourious Basterds (2009) rides this formula to an almost shameless extreme. That’s why I love and hate the dude’s work at the same time (besides the fact that he’s a proud Asiaphile). His films are cathartic but gratuitous, cinematically aware yet overly referential (the cinematic equivalent to The Game’s namedropping affliction). Always entertaining but, ultimately, hollowed out.

But something’s different with Basterds. With WW2 as his backdrop and Nazi-occupied France as his playground, he runs two parallel storylines. One’s been hyped up as the main plot:  Brad Pitt plays Aldo, a bloodthirsty southern American covert-ops commander leading a ragtag bunch of Jewish American soldiers on guerilla Nazi-hunts. The second follows Shosanna (Mélanie Laurent), a Jewish woman whose family is killed in the opening scene and later resurfaces as the owner of a cinema where Joseph Goebbels has chosen as the site of his new propaganda film.

The two storylines are woven together by a common archnemesis, the Jew-hunter Col. Hans Landa (played by scene-stealing Christoph Waltz). Shosanna’s cinema becomes a battleground (she declares to a German soldier who big-ups German actors  that “in France, we respect directors”). The war between nations becomes less about guns and more about culture, more specifically, cinema. French, German and Italian cinema history is pitted against each other leading to a literally explosive finale. The Basterds, Shosanna, her black lover and projectionist, Goebbels, and even a cartoonish Hitler himself all crash together at the film’s premiere screening. This is Tarantino’s boldest statement on both himself and his audience: are we that different from the Nazis giddily cheering the violence onscreen?

Without spoiling the film’s insane, magnificent climax (a spectacle even by Tarantino’s standards. one word: Scarface), I feel compelled to reference the last line of dialogue: “I think this might be my masterpiece.” On most days, for most films, I’d scoff at such masturbatory auteurism. But on this day, for this particular filmmaker, who poses few questions and provides no answers, I’d have to agree.
