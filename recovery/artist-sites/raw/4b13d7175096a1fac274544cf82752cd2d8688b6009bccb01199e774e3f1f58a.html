Tabu (F.W. Murnau, 1932)

May 13th, 2010 | No Comments »

F.W. Murnau, the director who made Nosferatu, also made a silent film about and starring Pacific Islanders called Tabu. (1932). It must’ve been “eye-opening” to its audience at the time. A title in the opening credits assures us that “All natives were cast in this film, including Half-castes and Chinese” to assure us that this is 100% genuine native shit. Same con that the dude who made Nanook of the North tried to play with the Eskimos.

Of course, we can look back now and pick apart how far off these exotifying white directors were back then (are things so different now?). But that’s no excuse to watch this film and not be fascinated by something about it. If you watch music videos these days, most can barely tell a story in three minutes. But Murnau, relying on just image and music, tells a story without no words for an hour-and-a-half. For this particular “silent,” it’s the syncing with the film’s score that drives it. For a tropical postcard of a movie, the silent form perhaps works best. It’s easier to suspend belief when there isn’t any dialogue to scrutinize.

At least, in theory. Most people of my generation have a hard digesting silent films. I suspect that it’s the music more than the old, grainy soft-focused image that turns them off. That’s why I’ve decided to re-score this film with some Blue Scholars instrumentals and share the results with you when I’m done.

DOWNLOAD: Prometheus Brown “Joe Arpaio” (Streaming/MP3)

May 1st, 2010 | 1 Comment »

Joe Arpaio, the self-proclaimed “America’s Toughest Sheriff” of Maricopa County, Arizona and open admirer of the Ku Klux Klan, is the architect behind his state’s new Racial Profiling Act (SB1070). He’s been doing his thing for years in AZ, except now he’s shielded from any legal repercussions. Nearly twenty years ago, Public Enemy recorded “By The Time I Get To Arizona,” in reference to the state’s refusal to recognize Martin Luther King, Jr.’s birthday as a holiday. Twenty years later, that Bomb Squad flip of that ol’ Mandrill sample still knocks, and those lyrics still resonate. Especially on the eve of a day like May 1. Historically known as International Workers’ Day, but in recent years also an annual march for immigrant rights, which we’ve participated in every year since 2005 (footage of our 2007 performance is cut into our “Back Home” music video).

Yesterday’s march in Seattle began at Judkins Playfield (St. Mary’s Church – 611 20th Ave. S), and we were in the Bay Area in solidarity performing at People’s Park. Isulong!
