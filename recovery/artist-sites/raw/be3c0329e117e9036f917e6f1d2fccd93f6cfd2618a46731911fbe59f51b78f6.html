Up In The Air (Jason Reitman, 2009)

January 5, 2010

Is there a Hollywood actor that has a higher batting average in the last decade than George Clooney (even with Leatherheads)? Watch any five minutes from Up In The Air (2009) and you’ll see why. Part of it is that he looks like a classic, old school American actor. He’d fit right in a Hitchcock or a ’40s noir film. Part of it is an uncanny ability to be convincingly charming and a complete asshole at the same time. That fucking smirk, man. He isn’t overly dramatic and never acts like he’s begging for an Oscar. But I suspect a rarer quality at work here, though. A look at his filmography reveals either politically relevant films (Good Night and Good Luck, Confessions of a Dangerous Mind, Michael Clayton) or stylized auteurist cinema (Solaris, The Oceans Series, Burn After Reading). Dude takes no fluffy roles.

Up In The Air gets props for setting its romance template against the backdrop of the actual economically-depressed America that exists outside the theater–something these love stories almost never do. Clooney plays Ryan Bingham, a man whose job is to fly all over the country to fire people for employers who don’t have the balls to do it themselves. The film’s opening, closing and frequent scenes in between are montages of emotional employee reactions. He gets paired with an ambitious younger professional job-terminator (Anna Kendrick) who challenges his individualist notions of relationships (and, in one fleeting instance, racism). Sensing his own job potentially being cut soon, he rethinks his fuck-buddy arrangement with a fellow traveling professional woman, Alex (Vera Farmiga). Somewhere in the mix, Young MC comes out of nowhere to perform “Bust A Move” while George sings along.

Without spoiling the ending, I’ll just say that I didn’t find it worthy of a film otherwise well-paced, beautifully-shot, intelligently written, and most of all, critical of the current economy (with humor). It dodges the expected, easy ending but manages to end on that high note required for a wide release. That same feeling I got watching the people in Jason Reitman’s other films (Thank You For Not Smoking, Juno) resolve their crises like good upper-middle class white people in movies are supposed to. All that aside, chalk this one up as another home run for George Clooney.
