Two In The Wave (Emmanuel Laurent, 2010)

August 23rd, 2010 | 2 Comments »

I caught Two In The Wave (2010) at Northwest Film Forum on a Saturday night right after hitting up Hempfest. Probably not the best time to catch a documentary in a small room with the hardest core of the city’s cinephiles, but I couldn’t pass up a film about the relationship and fall-out between Jean-Luc Godard and Francois Truffaut.

The first half of the film focuses on the duo’s debut features, The 400 Blows (1959) and Breathless (1960). After years of criticizing the Cannes Film Festival, Godard and Truffaut, who was banned a year before his arrival, become its darlings. Clips from these films, and a handful that followed, are interwoven with archival interview footage and narrated by director Emmanuel Laurent. We see Godard and Truffaut as homies bigging each other up in their reviews of each other’s films and even in the films itself. The two talk to each other through their characters. It’s cute. We see how they formed a bond living it up at Paris’s Cinematheque watching hella movies, writing for Cahiers Du Cinema, and eventually making films themselves. Boom, French New Wave. Excuse me, Nouvelle Vague.

After a few commercial flops following their initial hype, the breaking point: the Paris general strike and student walkouts of May 1968. In support of the strike, Godard and Truffaut make a call to shut down that bourgeois institution: Cannes. We see footage of Godard criticizing and self-criticizing cinema for not being in tune with the students’ and workers’ demands while Truffaut stands silently nearby. Gordard goes on to make Maoist agit-prop while Truffaut goes art for art’s sake. Godard criticizes Truffaut as a bourgeois filmmaker. Truffaut rejects the notion of film as a weapon to serve political ends. He goes Hollywood, Godard goes underground. Some class irony happens: Godard, of a rich intellectual family who was besties with Paul Valéry, turns to proletarian ideology while Truffaut, a working-class orphan who ran away from home and joined the army, comes to embrace the bourgeois inside him.

Among Godardians, there are two contending views regarding his “Maoist phase.” One, it was a great period of experimentation in film form and content or, two, that he went nuts. I was reminded of the latter viewpoint’s dominance as most of the audience giggled at the footage of a scrubby looking Godard, wearing a bathrobe, waxing political. A stark contrast to his early 60s image as a stylish Ray Ban-rocking hipster icon. Much of his filmography after Weekend (1967) is unfairly dismissed as experimental aberrations or undesirable propaganda; a recent touring JLG retrospective explicitly excluded these films.

The film’s final moments focus on Jean-Pierre Léaud, who played the autobiographical Antoine Donel in Truffaut’s films, as well as starring in some of Godard’s films. As the two directors start beefing, he’s torn between the two, until he stars in Truffaut’s Day For Night speaking lines that are not-so-subliminal jabs at Godard, who walks out during a screening. It’s like rap beef, but with diss scenes instead of diss songs.We hear a narration of Godard’s angry letters to Truffaut and Leaud, followed by Truffaut’s response. Thus, Truffaut gets the last word (and the last scene) before the film ends. The famous final freeze-frame ending of Truffaut’s The 400 Blows bookend the documentary. It’s almost like this film is saying Truffaut won.

Should films be made to serve a political end? Here, this question, as relevant then as it is now, is reduced to a personal squabble between two former buddies. In this subtle way, Two In The Wave’s story is more Truffaut’s than Godard’s: a story extracted from political context rather than confronting the contradictions head-on. Two In The Wave doesn’t break any new ground for those who are already familiar with Godard and Truffaut, but it’s an excellent introduction and reminder of a time when filmmakers took their work seriously enough, both politically and personally, to be worth beefing over.
