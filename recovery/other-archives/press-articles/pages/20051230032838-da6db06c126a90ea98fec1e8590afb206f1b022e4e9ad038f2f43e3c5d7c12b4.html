MUSIC
November 30 - December 6, 2005

Proletariat Blues
With a new EP and a demanding live schedule, Blue Scholars are the hardest-working hip-hop band in Seattle.

by Michaelangelo Matos


Geologic (left) and Sabzi of Blue Scholars.
(Ryan Schierling)

 		  

So far, Blue Scholars—the Seattle hip-hop duo of 23-year-old DJ Sabzi and MC Geologic, 25—have avoided that hoariest of pop-music clichés by not writing bitchy songs about The Road. But maybe they're due. The first time I interviewed them for this piece, they had just returned from a weeklong road trip; the group drove me in their touring van to the Moonlight Café in the International District for a convivial, entertaining lunch. Two days later, they would get in it again and travel down the coast for some more shows—right around the time I discovered that the microphone I'd conducted the interview with had been dead the entire time.

Over the phone from his South Seattle home, with occasional interruptions from his 15-month-old son ("He wants me to read to him; I'll just point at the words and talk to you like I'm reading them"), Geo notes the pressures of the group's workload. "Ever since the first album dropped, I don't think there's been a month where we didn't have any shows—at least one, [usually] more like four or five, sometimes eight or nine a month since February '04," he says.

The grind has yet to get to either man, probably because they're just beginning to master their terrain. Last year's Blue Scholars, which won the write-in Best Album category in 2004's Seattle Weekly Music Awards, showcased smart, light-fingered post–De La Soul beats and samples topped with a voice that seemed comfortable with its own gravity. When the duo reissued the album this summer, they added bonus tracks that did that rarest of things—they actually enhanced the album, particularly the playful "The Ave.," which featured a Sabzi beat that Prince Paul would give up GameCube for.

As you'd guess, "The Ave." is about the U District, where Blue Scholars' birth was preceded by the formation of S.H.O.W. (Student Hip-Hop Organization of Washington), a promotion/activist group led by future Scholars manager Marc Matsui that both Sabzi and Geo were involved with. "Marc and I tried to sneak into this [ID-only] Del the Funky Homosapien show at the Showbox in October '99," Geo says. "RKCNDY had just shut down and was being paved over and turned into a parking lot. The Teen Dance Ordinance was probably at its height, along with a sort of general antagonism among club promoters and owners toward hip-hop. [Marc and I] were both 19 at the time. I'd never met the dude before; he'd never met me. We both snuck into the kitchen in the back at the same time. We were sitting in this corner, hiding from everyone, and that's how we introduced ourselves. We stood there in awkward silence for two or three minutes, the coast was clear, and we pretty much ran onto the main floor."

After Matsui was caught and ejected (Geo escaped into the crowd and watched the show), he wrote an editorial for the University of Washington Daily. "There was this op-ed rant about how there was no scene for all ages hip-hop in Seattle anymore," says Geo. "At the end of this rant, [he wrote], 'Who's down to build a scene here on campus?' He expected to meet maybe one or two people to exchange CDs and talk about hip-hop. It turned out 10–15 people who responded initially, then maybe 10–15 more after that who were interested in helping out. It just snowballed." Within three months, the organization was putting on shows and nurturing local talent.

One of those new artists was a gangly Persian economics major who'd skipped a grade in public school. "Me and Marc were roommates at the time, and Marc was getting beat CDs from all kinds of people," Geo says. "I had to endure hearing a lot of garbage. Then one day I said, 'Wow, who's this? This is pretty tight.' He said, 'Oh, that's Sabzi.' I was like, 'No way—the dude in S.H.O.W. who helped us hand out flyers?' From then on, we'd go over to Sabzi's crib up in the U District, to his little attic bedroom. He'd play beats for us; Marc would scratch on the turntables, and I'd rhyme." That was in February 2002; by July, Geo and Sabzi had begun performing live.

After some awkward early steps, usually involving the hyperactive Sabzi stepping out from behind the turntables to hype the crowd—"I didn't mind [at the time]," Geo says, "but I've been seeing old performances on video, and I didn't realize how horrendous they were"—the duo quickly began to attract a buzz for their shows. "Overlapping the first part of Blue Scholars, I was active as a spoken-word performer, performing at least once a month as well," Geo says. The Filipino rapper was also part of his high school drama club—one of two nonwhite members, along with a Korean classmate. "The only parts I ever got were stage manager and stagehand, and the only parts he got were the butler or the chauffer," he says. "At the time, it never really crossed my mind to be like, 'That's racist,' but it's been seven or eight years now, and I can definitely say there was something there." Still, the experience taught him some valuable basics. "Every time I do a performance, I take notice of the six parts of the stage, [and] never turn your back to the crowd."

That onstage confidence is all over Blue Scholars' new The Long March EP, whose December 6 release the duo is playing Chop Suey to celebrate. The new disc is equally as easygoing as the debut, but with sharper production (Sabzi flips Hall and Oates' "Sara Smile" to brilliant effect on "The Long March") and lyrics, as on "Proletariat Blues" ("This tight-ass environment makes me want to hurl/Like watching Flavor Flav tongue-kissing homegirl") and "Southside Revival" ("You say you've got no time to study? People look/If you've got time to take a shit, you've got time to read a book"). It's a bold step toward . . .  well, Blue Scholars aren't entirely sure what's next—just that they're coming closer to it.

"We joke around a lot about this new era we're entering," Geo says. "We want to be a little more scientific about it, I guess, but keep that carefree spirit we had the first time. The conditions in our lives have drastically changed—we're not class-skipping college students anymore, making music. We'll probably have to take a step back [from playing live] for the next album." Which is only fair—they've earned it.

mmatos@seattleweekly.com

Blue Scholars play Chop Suey at 5 p.m. (all ages) and 9:30 p.m. (ID-only) Sat., Dec. 3. $8 all ages/$12 ID.
