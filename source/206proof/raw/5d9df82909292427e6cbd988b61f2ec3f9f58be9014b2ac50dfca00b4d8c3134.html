    Yes, it was a terrible call Husky nation

    September 6, 2008 8:15 PM
    Posted by ESPN.com's Ted Miller

    It was one of the worst judgment calls you will ever see -- no your eyes and instincts and sense of fairness didn't deceive you.

    When Washington quarterback Jake Locker ran for a 3-yard touchdown to put the Huskies within a converted PAT of tying their game with BYU, he threw the ball behind his neck so he could hug his teammates in order to properly enjoy a thrilling moment as the fans went bonkers.

    He was flagged for unsportsmanlike conduct for his celebration, a point of emphasis for officials this season.

    He certainly didn't break the spirit of the rule.

    The Huskies should have converted the resulting 35-yard PAT after 15 yards were walked off. It's a routine field goal distance.

    But they didn't.

    So they lost.

    And the result may irrevocably doom Huskies coach Tyrone Willingham.

    From Bob Condotta's Washington Football blog:

    Here is the official statement from referee Larry Farina on the penalty on Jake Locker at the end of the game:

    "After scoring the touchdown, the player threw the ball into the air and we are required, by rule, to assess a 15-yard unsportsmanlike conduct penalty. It is a celebration rule that we are required to call. it was not a judgment call."

    The rule in question is Rule 9, Section 2, Article 1 of the rule book.

    Section C of that rule states that "throwing the ball high into the air" is an unsportsmanlike act.

    Locker most certainly did not throw the ball "high into the air."

    The Pac-10 needs to formally explain this call. Or it should correct it, even if it can't change the result.

    And, in the oft-chance that somehow, some way, this rule can be twisted so Locker actually did something wrong based on the furthest periphery of the rule book, then that rule should be stricken from the rule book.

    This is a game. It's supposed to be fun. Big moments should be celebrated.

    There was absolutely zero element of taunting in the way Locker reacted. Period.

    The Huskies made so many mistakes in this game.

    Locker missed too many open receivers. Receivers dropped too many easy passes. The Huskies defense was again terrible -- BYU was an astounding 12 of 14 on third-down conversions.

    BYU looked like a better team, to be quite honest.

    But the ending transformed a highly entertaining game that should have been decided in overtime into a big, messy controversy.like we always do at this time!!! www.myspace.com/wizdom

    Don't worry, the NCAA will have this whole emotion thing under control
    By Tim Keown
    Page 2

    Updated: September 10, 2008, 1:28 PM ET

    The NCAA today announced it has outlawed all emotion on the football field. The timing of the announcement came as somewhat of a surprise. Originally, the prohibition on emotion was scheduled to take part in three phases, over a span of five years.

    The old plan called for the gradual removal of emotion beginning with (1) all expressions of joy; (2) all expressions of anger or frustration; and (3) all the subtle and less demonstrative emotions.

    The NCAA also announced it has formed a special committee -- comprised entirely of professed Stoics -- that will explore the possibility of prohibiting Division I football players from showing emotion off the field as well.

    The committee's report on off-field emotion is expected to be released at the conclusion of a long, thoughtful and -- of course -- thoroughly unemotional process. It could take years.

    The NCAA's decision comes in response to Saturday's game between Washington and BYU, which featured Washington quarterback Jake Locker's joyous reaction to scoring a dramatic touchdown on a broken play in the final seconds to bring his team to within one point of tying heavily favored BYU.

    Locker's touchdown run featured brilliant improvisation and spirited determination. Just as the game seemed to be over, Locker rallied his team down the field on the sheer force of his will. He brought the Husky Stadium crowd to its feet and sent them into delirium when he lunged his body across the goal line for the touchdown.

    However, his team was penalized 15 yards when he allowed emotion to creep into the mix by releasing the ball over his shoulder and hugging his teammates, who also appeared to be extremely happy by the developments. Washington subsequently had its 35-yard extra point blocked and BYU won, 28-27.

    After the game, the Pac-10 officials expressed surprise (classified as a subtle and less demonstrative emotion in the new bylaws) by the outrage (major emotion) that followed the ruling.

    The NCAA, in issuing its new policy, termed Locker's touchdown run "fully capable of standing on its own merits, without the benefit of Locker's ecstatic punctuation."

    The statement continued, "If we let this slide, pretty soon children all over the country will be tossing footballs over their shoulders in fits of exuberance after scoring potential game-tying touchdowns in the final seconds of a big game. This is simply unacceptable, and we feel it is our duty to keep it from happening again."

    According to several NCAA officials who asked not to be named, the implementation of the no-emotion rule was hastened by Locker's behavior after the play.

    "Videotape of Locker on the Washington sideline following the penalty showed him exhibiting behavior that indicated frustration, disgust and anger," one official said. "Clearly, he had not learned the lesson. Therefore, the NCAA has decided that further action is needed to prevent such occurrences in the future."

    Starting Saturday, 15-yard penalties will be assessed for any outward display of excitement, amusement, frustration, eagerness, disappointment, disbelief, anger, confusion, self-consciousness, ambition, bitterness, disgust and, of course, happiness.

    Minor irritation, skepticism, latent anxiety and quizzical facial expressions will be assessed a five-yard penalty. From here on, the lesser penalties will be assessed on what is to be termed "infractions of thought."

    "The job of the committee is to separate emotions from character traits and find a means of punishing the emotions while protecting the individual's right to be a person with his own unique set of character traits," the statement continued. "For instance, it might be difficult to differentiate between diffidence -- a known character trait -- and an outward display of fear -- an emotion now strictly forbidden. We're confident we've got good people working on it."
    A spokesman admitted there might be some gray area that could result in confusion (minor, depending on facial expression) or anger (major, always).

    "Love is a little tricky," the spokesman said. "We can't always detect it, but we will impose a penalty based on the assumption that our officials will know it when they see it."

    In a related development, the NCAA vowed to convene an exploratory committee to look into the issue of spontaneous displays of joy by spectators.

    "It's the last frontier," the spokesman said, "and we intend to go there."
