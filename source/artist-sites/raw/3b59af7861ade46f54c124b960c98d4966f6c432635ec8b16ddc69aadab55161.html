Police Beat (Robinson Devor, 2005)

Posted on July 8, 2008
Filed Under Films |

Police Beat (2005)
dir. Robinson Devor
81 min

When Police Beat was finished, I was convinced that two hours had passed, only to find out I was 40 minutes off target. For 80 minutes, the joint rides slow - but a good type slow. The kind slow necessary to paint two paintings at once without broad strokes: one of Seattle’s underbelly and another of one man’s struggle in a new country and career once promising, now teetering on misery (yeah, Seattle will do that to you). Almost sounds like a description of Bayani.

Z (Pape Sidy Niang) is a rookie Seattle bike cop and a lonely Senegalese immigrant. He narrates poetry in Wolof to his girlfriend in her absence while riding around protecting and serving the strange creatures that call Seattle home. The various incidents he tends to (murder, trees falling on old lady’s heads, S&M gone wrong) were taken from actual police beat records compiled by co-writer Charles Mudede, who writes a column of the same name, among other things, in Seattle’s alt-weekly The Stranger (and also wrote the first official review of our first album). 

Seattle is treated half-stereotypically, half-unexpectedly by director Robinson Devor and crew. The music is sad, it’s always cloudy, the scenery is beautiful and the people are weird and ugly. Overexposed film makes the city appear more dreamlike than it already is. The darks are deep, the light is blinding and the sky makes more cameos than a mixtape rapper (speaking of rappers - check for the Cancer Rising joint playing in the motel parking lot scene).  

But it’s Mudede’s narration and the series of bizarre crimes that keep the flick’s heart beating - at 75bpm. Riding through the most filmable parts of the city not named Pike Place or Pioneer Square, many of the scenes feel like travelogue without the traveling, and with a philosopher whispering in your ear shit like: “everybody has problems. even the flower has problems. humans are meant to solve problems” (I’m paraphrasing, though - apologies to Mr. Mudede).

I’m wondering if the thrill of seeing my own city in bright 16:9 blinded me from the film’s flaws, which I’m sure I’ll catch in later viewings. And I’ll let slide the fact that this story seemed to wholly avoid the Central District and South End (where there are probably more cops than anywhere else). And lastly in my ten years in the city, not once have I ever come across an African immigrant police officer. Part noir, part dark comedy, part neo-realism, part everything and nothing like the city it’s set in, Police Beat works like a slow ride - beautiful if you’re down to observe and feel, but excruciating if you’re in a hurry to be somewhere else.
