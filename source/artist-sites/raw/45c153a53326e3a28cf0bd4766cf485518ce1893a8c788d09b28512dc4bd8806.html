REVIEW: Simon del desierto (Luis Buñuel, 1965)

July 30th, 2010 | No Comments »

Si, Simón

I had no idea, as I watched Simon del desierto (1965) in an Anchorage hotel room after our show at midnight, that the calendar had landed right on the day director Luis Buñuel’s died 27 years ago (July 29, 1983). Fittingly strange coincidence for one of the strangest filmmakers ever. He wasn’t strange for the sake of being strange, though. Before surrealism got taken over by a bunch of uncommitted weirdos, it was an artistic movement that explicitly ridiculed and questioned religious and political authority. Buñuel, like all good Marxist artists of his time, was notorious for going in on the Catholic Church.

The film begins with a countryside crowd gathered around a pillar where a shaggy-locked guy named Simón (loosely based on 5th century Syrian saint Simeon Stylites, played by Claudio Brook) has lived for 6 years, 6 months, and 6 days. We don’t know where or when this is taking place. The priests and peasants salute him, rewarding him with an even bigger pillar. The pious, pitiful crowd gathers and asks his blessings. An amputee prays for his hands back, which Simón prays into existence. The poor man then slaps his kid, and everyone walks away. A repeated theme throughout Buñuel films: the institution corrupts not just the rulers, but the ruled.

Watching Simón contemplate his self-imposed isolation, we see that he, too, is conflicted. He has an ego-driven god complex fueled by his disgust for humanity, which he sees himself apart from. He refuses to stay with his sick mother, who camps out nearby. The devil comes three times to tempt him. To achieve this goal, Satan takes the seductive form of Sylvia Pinal, urging him to join her/it in the pursuit of the pleasures of the flesh. Story has it that Buñuel was restricted by time and budget and never actually completed the film, which explains the film’s abrupt, unexpected, almost silly ending. In this case, those unfortunate circumstances did the film well. Sometimes, with these art-house flicks, I’m ready for it to start wrapping up at the 45 minute mark. This time, it actually did. And that repressed Catholic upbringing in me felt guilty about it.
