Kidlat Tahimik: Mababangong Bangungot/Perfumed Nightmare (1978) and Turumba (1983)

Posted on July 24, 2008
Filed Under Film Reviews, Films, Philippine Cinema |

Mababangong Bangungot (Perfumed Nightmare) (1978)
dir. Kidlat Tahimik
93  min

Turumba (1981)
dir. Kidlat Tahimik
94 min

Kidlat Tahimik (Eric De Guia) is a weird dude, an anomaly - in a good way. Many have attempted to slap labels on him: experimental, alternative, art-house, even “political.” But labels like these tend to marginalize, deter mass audiences, and confine an artist’s work to a handful of connoisseurs who pick apart its form but can’t grasp its essence. The Latin filmmaker-driven Third Cinema movement’s primary objective was to liberate film from the bourgeois vicegrip and place it in the hands of indigenous or revolutionary (preferably both), anti-colonial filmmakers like Tahimik, who, in the spirit of that famous Marx quote, sought not merely to interpret the world, but to change it.  

His first two films, Mababangong Bangungot/Perfumed Nightmare (1978) and Turumba (1981), take cues from the Third Cinema movement approach (influenced by Fernando Solanas and Octavio Getino’s manifesto Towards a Third Cinema), standing (far) apart from mainstream Philippine cinema. Both films are set in the Philippine countryside during the height of Marcos’ martial-law regime, when even mainstream cinema was led by directors (Brocka, Bernal, De Leon) who dared to speak out with conventional narrative dramas.

Tahimik, however, goes beyond that - he doesn’t just lament imperialism’s sad results, but exposes its causes and lampoons its hubris. He is an anti-commercial filmmaker, using only what’s available out of the few resources he has, and  making up for the lack of funds by employing a unique delivery and narration style - a peasant with a camera who doesn’t give a fuck if the film stacks cheese, only that the story is told. 

Perfumed Nightmare (which won the International Critic’s Prize at the Berlin Film Festival in 1977) opens with a shot of a jeepney, the symbol and metaphor for all Filipino culture - improvisation and re-invention using few available resources, an ingenuity to breathe life into objects pronounced dead by those with endless supply. PN is constructed much like a jeepney, mixing real-life and staged shots in 8mm and reworking them with that Filipino soul.

Tahimik illustrates a formerly colonial (now, neocolonial) society and its illusory effects on the people trapped in its clutches. Not only directing but also playing the lead role, Tahimik introduces himself with a vignette about his town’s lone bridge, the villagers’ only link to the outside, rapidly-modernizing world. He listens to “Voice of America” talk-serial on the radio, is fascinated by technology and is the self-appointed president of the Werner von Braun Fan Club of Balian. A simple, smiling-ass man, Tahimik’s sincere geekiness reminds me of that one uncle many of us have. He’ll even look into the camera, acknowledging the audience long before legendary actor Mark-Paul Gosselaar (as Zack Morris) made it his trademark.

One day, he gets a rare opportunity to travel to Europe. Initially, he is overwhelmed by the grandeur of developed, modernized metropolises that third-world countries like the Philippines aspire to have one day. Then, disillusion. He observes how, on the flipside of technological achievement, ordinary people are displaced, their needs unanswered by a system marching forward no matter who’s in the way. This is the nightmare: the loss of a people’s livelihood and culture sugarcoated by the seductive illusion of progress. He returns home, traumatized by the erosion of his lifelong dreams. Changed, he realizes that while progress is dope, (neo)colonization is wack, and too many bear the burden for the glory of a few. 

Critics have commented on PN’s crude, minimal style - some with elitist condescension, others with pat-a-brotha-on-the-head praise. I was charmed by the unusual narration style, which is neither fully documentary or fully narrative, but more like an experimental, audiovisual diary. It’s the antithesis of travelogue - not the tourist’s story, but the story of one of the many nameless, faceless people seen existing as scenery for a traveling colonizer’s postcard for other traveling colonizers. The bridge, thus, is a metaphor for the links made between one’s culture and modernization - a mutual exchange may be possible when the link is respected by travelers on both sides, but will break under excess weight. 

So, imperalism’s excesses are in Turumba’s crosshairs as well. More linear and cohesive than PN, Tahimik’s second film (which he actually began production on before PN) can almost be considered a sequel, or companion film to the first. 

It opens with a young boy, Kadu, narrating the enthusiasm and innocence he and his townmates experience during the yearly Turumba festival, a Catholic celebration. The camera functions less as a floating, invisible narrator than as a fellow unnamed, unseen villager’s eyes, capturing footage of real-life events, cut with staged conversations and crafted together to make something new (again, the Jeepney metaphor).

Both PN and Turumba wear comedy like a barong - you can still see the scars underneath the laughter. Kadu’s upbringing in the countryside borders on a poverty that Tahimik portrays not with a distant pity or guilt, but a compassion familiar enough to draw humor out of tragedy: how they’ve adjusted to frequent blackouts and make new things from scraps (the town blacksmith announces, “I hammered this machete out of a Mercedes Benz!”). For the first 30 minutes of the film, it’s all music and laughter in the midst of poverty, familiar images of the countryside - a perfect set up for an interruption.

Kadu’s lola makes papier mache animals every year for Turumba, using the meager profits to support the family. This year (1971), it all changes when a German export-buyer sees Kadu selling the crafts for hella cheap and offers to buy all 300 of them (leaving none for the townsfolk). Then, she orders 500 more for Oktoberfest, then 25,000 for the 1972 Munich Olympics. She handpicks Romy, the town kantor and Kadu’s father, to “rationalize production.” In one year’s time, the yearly one-family tradition transforms into a multi-hut operation, then a full-blown sweatshop factory. Time, once free, is now held prisoner by business and long-standing traditions such as the Turumba may be next.

Like Kidlat Tahimik’s character in PN, Kadu and others lament the loss of time spent living rather than working. He asks his father “when is it time for music?” Kadu’s lola reminds me of my two grandmothers (Inang and Cacang, RIP) - clinging to tradition not stubbornly, but with quiet devotion and skepticism of “progress” (she digs The Beatles, but also reminds Kadu “don’t forget about tinikling”). Lola, along with other minor characters, supply challenging views on the encroachment of business in the countryside. Some are grateful that their children have jobs. Others, such as the blacksmith and the dressmaker, rightfully feel threatened. The film ends without a resolution, not as a postmodern gimmick like many “hip” artsy films do nowadays, but as a statement that, yes, this is actually happening in the Philippines, and, no, there is no happy ending (if there is, it’s up to us to write and direct it).

While PN got that first-film amateur charm, Turumba is a technically superior film. But alas, the progress in technology and production critiqued in both films affects the filmmaker himself: the former film, with all its technical flaws, possesses a soul that’s muted in the latter. PN takes more risks with narrative form and Kidlat Tahimik is magnetic in the unconventional, autobiographical role, out-charming many of these OA types. Turumba’s strength can also be viewed as a weakness (by conventional film standards): the players are all non-professional actors (or, if they are, which I doubt, then they should find another job) conversing in obviously-staged scenes. But that they’re also real people just doing what they do (with a few director’s notes), giving the story a realism that eludes other so-called “realist” dramas.

In both PN and Turumba, Kidlat Tahimik narrowly avoids the nostalgia trap. It’s commonplace to see works of art lament capitalism, wishing things were “back to the way they were” before the transition from feudal to capital. Such idealism is not only backwards in thinking, but serves nothing to a liberation movement. But in a country undergoing that traumatic transition right now, that feeling does exist, and Tahimik, in faithful commitment to reality, makes it part of the story, but not the story itself. Both films fall short of proposing an alternative, but their bolo-sharp analysis and critique of imperialism have much to offer the masa in their need for more films that reflect them as they are - a critique made with more heart than mind, but a critique nonetheless, and just as necessary.
