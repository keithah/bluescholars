Le Mépris (Jean-Luc Godard, 1963)

August 8, 2008

Le Mépris (Contempt, 1963)
dir. Jean-Luc Godard
98 min
France

As exciting as it has been to tour the country (and parts of Canada) for the first time, I’m really missing Seattle this week. If I were home, I’d definitely be catching some of SIFF’s screenings of nine of Jean-Luc Godard’s 1960’s French New Wave (Nouvelle Vague) films, even though some of his more overtly Marxist work (La Chinoise, Le Gai savoir) is conveniently excluded. I’ve seen about 1/4 of Jean-Luc Godard’s extensive filmography (60+ features spanning nearly 40 years) and though I can’t say that I enjoyed all of them, his films are easily among the most artistically and politically challenging works I’ve seen.

As one of the prominent figures of the French Novelle Vague film movement, JLG’s work paid homage to and rejected cinematic norms at the time, injected radical politics and philosophy, and included innovations in editing and dialogue that have influenced later generations (notably, Scorsese, Tarantino, Fassbinder). Le Mépris  (Contempt, 1963), which opens SIFF’s JLG run, was his sixth feature and first big-budget project, and, retrospectively, a turning-point in his career as his following projects pushed film experimentation and radical politics even further.

It’s a well-dressed satire – a movie about making a movie. In Godard’s hands, its an opportunity to make a statement – about capitalism, love, filmmaking, art. Locating the narrative behind the camera works perfectly for this intent, with Godard addressing both his grievances and love for filmmaking through a series of philosophical and cultural-namedropping dialogue. Surface-level, it’s simplified Godard fare, almost as if he’s saying, sure, I’ll play your big-studio game, all the while clowning its excess and emptiness. It’s a clever irony, considering all the hype that surrounded Le Mepris‘ production and the dual expectation for the film to make money on one hand and make a sharp political statement on the other.

Godard’s work before Le Mepris featured lesser-known actors and real folks. Long resisting, then finally (begrudgingly?) accepting big studios’ suggestions to use “marketable” actors, the cast is assembled clowningly: playwright/screenwriter Paul (Michel Piccoli), his aspiring actress wife, Camille (uber-racist Brigitte Bardot in her prime), director Fritz Lang (as himself!) and American asshole producer Jeremy Prokosch (Jack Palance, playing a role that George W. Bush seems to have jacked his glum swagger from). 

They are working on a film adaptation of Homer’s Odyssey, filmed in some breathtaking part of Italy by the sea. Contradictions emerge: between Lang and Prokosch (artistry vs. profit), and, most notably between Paul and Camille, who argue for about half the film using quotes from Bertolt Brecht and Dante. Frustratingly, Camille never fully explains why she started to feel contempt for Paul – only that she starts out loving him, got involved in the film, spent 30 minutes alone with Prokosch, and suddenly started hating him.  

Loaded as Le Mepris is, the story we don’t see onscreen might be even more intriguing than the film itself. Godard supposedly got into it with his producers as well as with Palance during production. Much of the Paul/Camille dynamic seems to be drawn from Godard’s own experiences with Anna Karina, his wife and lead actor in many of his films. In the end, though Le Mepris might have not been what his producers hoped for, it has become a rare moment in cinema where the director plays the game while defying the system at the same time.
