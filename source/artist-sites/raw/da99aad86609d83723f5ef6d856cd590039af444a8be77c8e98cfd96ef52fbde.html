SIFF Blog: Tirador/Slingshot (Brillante Mendoza, 2008)

Posted on May 28, 2008
Filed Under Film Reviews, Philippine Cinema, SIFF 2008 |

Tirador/Slingshot
dir. Brillante Mendoza
88 min 
Philippines 

My first 2008 SIFF film is an entry from The Philippines - Slingshot (Tagalog: Tirador), written and directed by Brillante Mendoza. Sadly, I’m not familiar with Philippine cinema other than cheesy comedies or ABS-CBN (Hollywood-equivalent) standard fare. But I wasn’t gonna miss the chance to see people who look like me on the big screen of what is probably the whitest theater in the whole city (Harvard Exit, right off Broadway)!

Slingshot follows the daily life of several characters who live in one building of a Manila slum, probably not too different from the neighborhood Ka Bel lived in. Stylistically, it looks like unedited documentary footage. Many indie films have used the handheld digital camera approach, but here it perfectly and intentionally captures the commotion of the barrio. Ambient noise (cars, voices, music) always seems to hover slightly above the dialogue, and the viewer is never made to feel comfortable throughout.

A sophist-type might call this film existential - I call it truthful. Anyone familiar with the Philippines will tell you that hope is in short supply (no wonder we’re leaving our homeland at the rate of 1,000+ a day). While other films fall into the trap of romanticizing the third world slum, Mendoza de-romanticizes it. Conversations end abruptly, every comedic moment is also tragic, and when someone (anyone) runs, the camera follows. There is no “poor noble” or any of that rose-that-grew-from-concrete type shit here.

Contrasting petty thieves and drug-addicts alongside corrupt politicians and violent police, Slingshot poses a question: who are the real criminals of Philippine society? Those who prey on whole communities (the Church and the State), or those who prey on other individuals (”criminals”)? Although the film lets reality speak for itself rather than push forth an overt political statement, Mendoza doesn’t ride the fence on this question. Cutting in preists’ and politicians’ speeches, promises and poster slogans in between scenes of barrio life, we see a subtle indictment of the institutions that vie for the loyalty of a people they have continued to fail.

    Thanks Ivan. I liked City of God. Alot, actually. But moreso as a fictional story rather than the “real” film its celebrated to be.
