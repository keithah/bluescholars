Love and Arms

Posted on April 22, 2008
Filed Under Culture, Politics |

From International Examiner, Reconnecting Filipinos through love of art
Category/Issue: Arts & Entertainment, Volume 35 No. 08 (April 12, 2008):

[James] Ardena explains that upon his departure he had some issues with [isangmahal arts] kollective and other National Democratic Front Organizations associated with the group such as Pinay Sa Seattle and Anakbayan.

He says, “One of the things that is central to these organizations today is their belief in armed revolution and to have an armed resistance towards the white supremacist capitalist patriarchy.”

Ardena saw this as sad and disappointing seeing as these ideas changed from the kollective’s initial emphasis on the idea of love and self-actualization and liberation through love.

Although we may differ in what constitutes a legitimate “revolution,” I still consider James Ardena an influential mentor and a pillar in the community. The walls inside my home are adorned with his distinct Filipino-themed artwork. Most importantly, he was a co-founder of isangmahal arts kollective, of which I was an active member from 1999 to around 2004-2005, when Blue Scholars started taking off. Like most young arts organizations with no formal organizational and financial structure, we had our triumphs and struggles. Among the latter was a question of what “liberation” actually meant. 

For some, including James and many of the original members, it meant “self-love.” I was never really sure what that meant, but it felt right at the time. Post-modern literature and political posturing in form (but not essence) was the dominant theory and practice among members. Liner notes of our Live CD Recording (2001) sported a quote by J. Krishnamurti. I grew out my hair and wrote lots of spacey poetry during this time.

Then, some of us began participating in political organizing. For some of us who came later into the collective, including myself, true liberation meant linking ourselves with the current movement for National Democracy in the Philippines and politically educating, mobilizing and organizing around campaigns that addressed issues both here and back home. While we had no disagreement with the original ideals of love and compassion in all of our work, artistic or otherwise, we saw its failings as a “movement” to address real isms. 

In truth, isangmahal was neither fully post-modern or fully revolutionary or fully anything - except for a “safe space” where Filipino artists who were marginalized in whatever medium they practiced found collaborative partners and a listening audience. The simultaneous beauty and downfall of the collective was that it comprised of Filipinos (and a few non-Filipinos) who had differing political viewpoints but recognized the necessity of the space. A family is a family, no matter what disagreements they may have, right?

In the IE article, the author uses a quote from James that puts forth the notion that “love” and “revolution” are mutually exclusive - perpetuating myths that exist on all sides: that an armed resistance is devoid of love, and that any compassionate movement must reject arms, even in self-defense (which goes for essentially all legitimate liberation movements). The current Philippine administration would love nothing more than its people to pursue self-love rather than land reform and political and economic self-determination. 

Furthermore, the article mistakenly ties Anakbayan and Pinay Sa Seattle to the National Democratic Front (not to be confused with the greater National Democratic, or ND, movement), which neither organization is  a member of. Rather, these organizations are members of BAYAN-USA, an extension of BAYAN, which comprises unarmed mass organizations who use only legal means of fighting for genuine democracy. Rather than taking up arms ourselves, we only recognize and understand any oppressed people’s right to self-defense.

It has been a tactic of the Philippine president Gloria Macapagal-Arroyo’s government (and reactionary governments throughout history) to tag legal, unarmed progressive individuals and organizations as armed threats in order to justify using violence to suppress them. Unfortunately, this article falls for the same tactic.

Lastly, the article misrepresents other contributors to the exhibit as members of isangmahal arts kollective. In fact, the majority of artists in the exhibit are current or former members of Pinay Sa Seattle and Anakbayan.

Perhaps not enough time has passed for me to thoroughly assess my time with isangmahal or to address some lingering issues. But I remain grateful for all the relationships I’ve formed through the collective and the space that allowed me to find my voice and the confidence to pursue my art beyond the confines of my notebook.

Ernesto Guevara said a “true revolutionary is guided by great feelings of love.” While I believe “self-love”  is important, it’s love for others that’s paramount to any movement. And for myself and others who are active in the ND movement, there’s no greater way to show that love than to risk your own life to fight against state repression and organize for genuine freedom.
