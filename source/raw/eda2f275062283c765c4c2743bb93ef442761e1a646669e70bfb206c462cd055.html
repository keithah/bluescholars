PRESS REVIEWS

But over here, we review the press. Nothing against the writers - in fact, I used to do interviews and review records in publications (got up in URB, son!). It was fun heaping subjective opinion on somebody else's art and possibly influencing a reader's opinion. Ah, the power of the media.

Music critics and writers maintain that their reviews and articles are an integral part of the game. I agree. We need them to provide concrete criticism from which we artists can then up our game. But who will be there to help these writers up their game? Usually, it's their Editors-in-Chief. But they need to be checked, too. Welcome to our corner of the media, where the reviewers are reviewed themselves, held against the standards they hold us by.

Brokenspindle.com
Blue Scholars LP Review

****1/2		  
Had I done my Top 50 countdown of 2004 a bit later, this record would probably be in the top 10. And it's coming out of left field to sky rocket to the top. What is it? It's the Blue Scholars and their stunning debut self-titled album.

The hip hop/rap group from Seattle mixes smooth politically influenced lyrics by Geologic, and the awesome instrumentation by Sabzi to create one of the most worthwhile rap album I've heard in quite some time. The Roots and others have done a great job using organic sound with their rapping ability, and the Blue Scholars are no different. Sabzi's use of keyboards, guitar, drum beats, and random other sounds make the record's ecletic sound not only a support to the rap, but a co-star on the album.

Seattle Times
"Hip-hop movement jolts young voters"
July 30, 2004

****
  
As a boy coming of age in Seattle's Filipino community, George Quibuyen had no voice. At parent-teacher conferences, they pondered his "pathological shyness" while he grooved to rap by LL Cool J and Run DMC on tapes borrowed from older kids.

It was just a hobby at first, like Nintendo or the Garbage Pail Kids, but Quibuyen couldn't help but admire the confidence of MCs who spoke their minds without worries. He'd penned a few cheesy love poems for the girls, but otherwise his rhymes were largely imitative.

Then, at 14, he heard "Illmatic," Nas' 1994 album about life in the projects.

“Isn’t it strange how we estrange ourselves from our neighbors?” asks MC Geologic over DJ/producer Sabzi’s snare taps, plucked guitars, and opening-credits horns on “Blue School,” the second track on the duo’s excellent self-titled album, released this past February. The Stranger
Blue Scholars LP Review
March 2004

*****
  
"Blue Scholars is an experiment in the hiphop tradition of the potent MC-DJ connection," claims the first sentence of the bio posted on the Seattle-based duo's website. "DJ/Producer Sabzi's funk-inflected instrumentals provide a gritty, percussion-heavy texture that blends curiously with emcee Geologic's poetic mic presence." The proof of this statement's accuracy can be found on Blue Scholars' self-titled debut CD. The two do form a "potent MC-DJ connection." However, if separated, both Sabzi's beats and Geologic's raps could easily stand alone as complete works of hiphop art.

Blue Scholars self-titled debut is one of those. Yeah, it's on some conscious, backpacker type vibe, but emcee Geologic is a rhyme animal and DJ/producer Sabzi production reminds you - in a very good way - of Pete Rock...
